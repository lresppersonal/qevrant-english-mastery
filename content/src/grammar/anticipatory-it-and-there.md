## core | core | Core concept
English likes to keep the beginning of a sentence light and to put long or new information at the **end**. The "empty" subjects *it* and *there* make this possible. *It* stands in for a long subject that moves to the end; *there* introduces something new.
!rule *It* + *be* + adjective + *to*-infinitive / *that*-clause: ***It** is difficult **to find a flat***. *There* + *be* + new noun phrase: ***There** are three reasons for this.*
> ex: To find a flat in central Vienna is difficult. → **It** is difficult to find a flat in central Vienna. || the heavy subject moves to the end
> ex: A link between diet and memory exists. → **There is** a link between diet and memory. || new information introduced naturally
Russian often leaves the subject out (*Важно проанализировать…*), but English always needs a subject: *It is important to analyse…*

## overview | overview | Overview and purpose
*To find a flat in central Vienna is difficult* is grammatical but sounds heavy. Native speakers say ***It** is difficult to find a flat in central Vienna.* The empty subject *it* "anticipates" the real subject, which moves to the end. Similarly, ***There** are three reasons for this* introduces new information more naturally than *Three reasons for this are*. Mastering these two dummy subjects is one of the clearest differences between intermediate and advanced writing.

## theory | theory | How it works: end-weight and end-focus
Two principles of English information structure explain both constructions:
- **End-weight**: long, complex constituents go at the end of the clause, where the listener has already processed the verb.
- **End-focus**: new and important information goes at the end, where sentence stress naturally falls.
A that-clause, wh-clause or to-infinitive subject is usually both long and new, so English moves it to the end and fills the subject position with **it** (*extraposition*). When we introduce something new into the discourse, English uses **there + be** (*existential there*), placing the new noun phrase after the verb.
!rule *It* refers forward to a clause; *there* introduces the existence or presence of something. *~~There is important to check~~* is wrong; *It is important to check* is right.

## form | form | Formation: the main patterns
#### Anticipatory it
| Pattern | Example |
| it + be + adjective + to-infinitive | *It is **essential** to verify the sources.* |
| it + be + adjective + that-clause | *It is **likely** that rates will rise.* |
| it + be + adjective + wh-clause | *It is **unclear** whether the policy works.* |
| it + be + noun + that/to | *It is **a pity** that you missed it. It was **a mistake** to wait.* |
| it + verb + object + that/to | *It **surprised** me that she agreed. It **took** years to finish.* |
| it + passive reporting verb + that | *It **is believed** that the fire started accidentally.* |
| verb + it + adjective/noun + to/that (object it) | *I **find it** hard to concentrate. We **consider it** essential that ...* |
| it + seem/appear/happen + that | *It **seems** that the files were lost.* |
#### Existential there
| Pattern | Example |
| there + be + noun phrase (+ place/time) | *There **is** a problem with the data.* |
| there + modal + be | *There **may be** other explanations.* |
| there + seem/appear/tend + to be | *There **seems to be** a misunderstanding.* |
| there + be + noun + -ing / past participle | *There were students **waiting** outside. There were two people **injured**.* |
| there + passive reporting verb + to be | *There **are said to be** over a thousand species.* |
| there + verb of existence (literary) | *There **remains** one question. There **exist** several models.* |
!tip The verb after *there* agrees with the following noun: *There **is** a reason / There **are** two reasons.* In informal speech *there's* is often used with plurals (*there's lots of people*), but not in writing.

## uses | uses | Main uses
#### 1. Evaluating with adjectives
*It is **crucial / doubtful / worth noting / widely accepted** that ...* lets the writer evaluate an idea before stating it. This is central to academic stance.
#### 2. Distancing and hedging with reporting verbs
*It **is often argued** that ..., It **has been suggested** that ..., It **would seem** that ...* present claims without naming a source or committing fully.
#### 3. Introducing topics and listing
*There are several factors to consider. There is little evidence that ... There is no doubt that ...*
#### 4. Time and distance
*It's two years since we met. It takes three hours to get there. It's a long way to the coast.*
#### 5. Object it
After *find, make, consider, think, believe* the infinitive or that-clause cannot directly follow the verb: *I find **it** difficult to explain* (~~I find difficult to explain~~). *The new rules make **it** easier to apply.*

## examples | examples | Examples in context, explained
> ex: It is worth noting that the sample was drawn from urban areas only. || evaluation before the point, a useful limitation statement
> ex: It has been estimated that a third of food is wasted. || impersonal passive: source not named
> ex: There is growing concern about the use of facial recognition. || existential: introduces a new topic
> ex: There appears to be no simple solution. || *there* + *appear to be*: tentative
> ex: The software makes it possible to track changes in real time. || object *it* after *make*
> ex: It was not until the 1960s that the theory was accepted. || *it*-cleft for emphasis (compare with extraposition)
> ex: There remain several unanswered questions. || formal existential with *remain*

## contrasts | contrasts | Comparison with related structures
| Pair | Difference |
| *It is said that X* vs *X is said to* | two impersonal passives: *It is said that the castle is haunted* / *The castle is said to be haunted*. The second makes the castle the topic. |
| *there is said to be* | the existential version: *There is said to be gold in the hills.* |
| extraposition vs *it*-cleft | *It is surprising **that** she left* (extraposition: *it* = that she left) vs *It was **Maria** **who** left* (cleft: focuses on Maria; remove *it was ... who* and a full sentence remains). |
| *it* vs *there* | *It is a problem* (the situation already mentioned is a problem) vs *There is a problem* (a problem exists; new information). |
| *it takes* vs *I need* | *It took me an hour to finish* (time required) vs *I needed an hour to finish*. Both correct; *it takes* is more idiomatic for time. |

## register | register | Register and stylistic nuance
!register Academic writing relies heavily on anticipatory *it* to evaluate and hedge (*It is likely / possible / clear / important that*). Overuse, however, produces a string of empty openings. Vary with personal or noun subjects: *These results suggest ...*, *A plausible explanation is ...*.
In speech, *there's* + plural and the object-*it* pattern are frequent; existential *there* with verbs other than *be* (*there exists, there arose*) is formal or literary.

## errors | errors | Common mistakes
!! Is important to analyse the results. || It is important to analyse the results. || English sentences need a subject; *it* fills the empty position.
!! There is important to remember the context. || It is important to remember the context. || *It* anticipates a clause; *there* introduces a noun phrase.
!! I find difficult to concentrate in the library. || I find it difficult to concentrate in the library. || After *find, make, consider* + adjective, add *it* before the adjective.
!! It exists a strong link between diet and health. || There is a strong link between diet and health. || Use *there is / there are* to say that something exists.
!! There is many reasons for this. || There are many reasons for this. || The verb agrees with the noun that follows *there*.
!! It is said the fire to have started at night. || The fire is said to have started at night. / It is said that the fire started at night. || These are two separate patterns; do not mix them.
## advanced | advanced | Advanced applications
#### Extraposition from noun phrases
Long post-modifiers can also move to the end: *A rumour spread **that the CEO had resigned**.* (instead of *A rumour that the CEO had resigned spread*). The result is lighter and clearer.
#### Combining hedges
*It would **appear** that there **may** be some grounds for concern.* Multiple hedges signal caution but can make a claim vanish; use them in proportion to the strength of the evidence.
#### There + negative quantifiers for strong claims
*There is **no** evidence that ..., There is **little** reason to believe ..., There is **every** reason to expect ...* give a confident, compact verdict.

## summary | summary | Summary
- Move long subject clauses to the end with anticipatory *it*: *It is clear that ...*.
- Use object *it* after *find, make, consider*: *I find it hard to ...*.
- Use *there + be* to introduce new things; the verb agrees with the following noun.
- Impersonal passives: *It is said that X* / *X is said to* / *There is said to be*.
- Do not confuse extraposition with the *it*-cleft.

- There are grounds for concern.
- It is clear that there are grounds for concern.

> *Would appear* and *may* combine two hedges.

### [mc] @contrasts C2
Which sentence is an *it*-cleft, not extraposition?
+ It was the timing that caused the problem.
- It was obvious that the timing was wrong.
- It is unclear whether the timing matters.
- It surprised everyone that the timing changed.
> Remove *It was ... that* and a full sentence remains: *The timing caused the problem.*

Rewrite with *There*: *Gold is said to be buried under the old mill.*
= There is said to be gold buried under the old mill.
> Existential *there* + passive reporting verb + *to be*.

### [context] @examples C2
Choose the most natural academic sentence.
+ It is worth noting that the data come from a single region.
- That the data come from a single region is worth noting it.
- There is worth noting that the data come from a single region.

Move the heavy clause to the end: *A report that the company was in financial difficulty appeared in the press.*
= A report appeared in the press that the company was in financial difficulty.

## overview | overview | Overview and purpose
Compare *I understand **the problem*** with *I understand **why the system failed***. The clause *why the system failed* fills the same position as the noun phrase *the problem*. That is a **noun clause** (also called a nominal or content clause). Noun clauses let you treat a statement, a question or a fact as a single unit you can then describe, evaluate or argue about: ***What the data show** is **that** the effect is temporary.*

## theory | theory | How it works: three families of noun clause
1. **That-clauses** package a statement: *(that) the experiment worked*.
2. **Wh-clauses** package an open question or an unknown: *what happened, why prices rose, how the scheme works*.
3. **Whether/if-clauses** package a yes/no question: *whether the policy is fair*.
Because they are no longer real questions, wh- and whether-clauses use **statement word order**: *I wonder why **she left*** (not *~~why did she leave~~*). This is the same logic as indirect questions.
!rule Noun clauses can stand where nouns stand: **subject** (*That he resigned surprised everyone*), **object** (*I doubt whether it matters*), **complement** (*The problem is that we lack data*), **after a preposition** (*It depends on **what** you mean*) and **in apposition** to a noun (*the fact **that** he lied*).

## form | form | Formation: positions and patterns
| Position | That-clause | Wh-clause | Whether-clause |
| subject | ***That** the plan failed* is clear. | ***What** she said* was unexpected. | ***Whether** it works* remains to be seen. |
| object | I believe **(that)** it's true. | Tell me **where** you live. | I doubt **whether / if** it's true. |
| complement | The truth is **that** we forgot. | That's **why** I called. | The question is **whether** we can afford it. |
| after preposition | (not possible: use *the fact that*) | It depends on **how** you do it. | We argued about **whether** to stay. |
| after noun (apposition) | the belief **that** money brings happiness | the question **of how** to pay | the question **(of) whether** to continue |
| after adjective | I'm sure **(that)** she knows. | I'm not clear **what** you want. | I'm not sure **whether** he's coming. |
!tip *That* can be omitted after common reporting verbs and adjectives in informal style (*I think you're right*), but not when the clause is the subject (***That** he lied is obvious*).

## uses | uses | Main uses
#### 1. Packaging an idea as the topic
Starting with a noun clause puts the idea in focus: ***How** universities should be funded** is a recurring debate.* Long subject clauses are often moved to the end with anticipatory *it* (see that lesson): *It is unclear **whether** the scheme works.*
#### 2. Reporting and evaluating
Reporting verbs (*argue, claim, suggest, demonstrate*) take that-clauses; verbs of uncertainty (*wonder, ask, doubt, investigate, establish*) take wh- or whether-clauses: *The study investigates **whether** sleep affects recall.*
#### 3. Talking about facts with prepositions
Prepositions cannot be followed directly by a that-clause. Use ***the fact that***: *Despite **the fact that** it rained, ...*; *She was annoyed **by the fact that** nobody replied.* (Or rephrase: *Although it rained ...*)
#### 4. Nominal relative clauses
*What, whatever, whoever, wherever* create clauses meaning "the thing(s) that / anyone who": *I'll do **whatever** it takes.* These are covered in detail in the lesson on nominal relative clauses.

## examples | examples | Examples in context, explained
> ex: **That** the climate is changing is no longer disputed. || that-clause as subject (formal)
> ex: It is no longer disputed **that** the climate is changing. || more natural: extraposed with *it*
> ex: The report does not explain **how** the figures were calculated. || wh-clause as object, statement order
> ex: Much depends on **whether** the government acts quickly. || whether-clause after a preposition (*if* is not possible here)
> ex: The assumption **that** growth is always good needs examining. || appositive that-clause after a noun
> ex: I'm not convinced **(that)** this is the right approach. || adjective + that-clause, *that* optional in speech
> ex: **Why** some languages die out while others thrive is a complex question. || wh-clause as a long subject

## contrasts | contrasts | Comparison with related structures
| Pair | Difference |
| appositive *that* vs relative *that* | *the news **that** she had won* (= content of the news; *that* cannot be replaced by *which*) vs *the news **that/which** she brought* (relative: *that* is the object of *brought*). |
| *whether* vs *if* | Both introduce yes/no clauses after verbs (*I don't know whether/if ...*). Only *whether* is possible: as subject, after prepositions, before *to*-infinitive (*whether to go*) and with *or not* immediately after (*whether or not*). |
| *what* vs *that* | *what* = the thing which: *What he said was true.* *That* introduces a fact: *That he said it was true* (= the fact of his saying it). |
| wh-clause vs direct question | *Do you know where **she lives**?* not *~~where does she live~~*. |
| *the fact that* vs *that* | After prepositions and as a heavy subject, *the fact that* is required or clearer. |

## register | register | Register and stylistic nuance
!register Subject that-clauses (*That X is true is clear*) are formal and can sound heavy; most writers prefer *It is clear that X*. Subject wh-clauses (*What matters is ...*) are common in both speech and writing as a focusing device.
Overuse of *the fact that* makes prose wordy: *Due to the fact that* → *because*; *In spite of the fact that* → *although*. Keep it where a noun phrase is genuinely needed.

## errors | errors | Common mistakes advanced learners make
> x: I don't know what does he want. || I don't know what he wants.
> x: Despite that it was late, we continued. || Despite the fact that it was late, ... / Although it was late, ...
> x: The question is if we should continue. || The question is whether we should continue.
> x: What I want to say that education matters. || What I want to say is that education matters.
> x: I'm not sure about if it is correct. || I'm not sure whether it is correct.
> x: The news which he had resigned shocked everyone. || The news that he had resigned shocked everyone.

## advanced | advanced | Advanced applications
#### Pseudo-clefts with noun clauses
***What** the evidence suggests **is that** the effect fades over time.* The wh-clause sets up expectation; the that-clause delivers the point. This structure is powerful in conclusions but loses force if repeated.
#### Mandative that-clauses
After verbs and nouns of demand and recommendation, formal English uses the base form: *The committee recommends **that** the policy **be** reviewed.* (See the subjunctive lesson.)
#### Noun clauses and hedging
Embedding a claim in a that-clause lets you attribute and qualify it: *It **appears** that ..., There is **some evidence** that ..., One **possible** explanation is that ...*. This is a central technique of academic stance.

## summary | summary | Summary
- Noun clauses function as subject, object, complement, prepositional object or appositive.
- That-clauses report facts and claims; wh- and whether-clauses report unknowns and questions.
- Use statement word order inside wh- and whether-clauses.
- After prepositions use wh-/whether-clauses or *the fact that*, never a bare that-clause.
- *Whether* (not *if*) as subject, after prepositions, before to-infinitives and with *or not*.

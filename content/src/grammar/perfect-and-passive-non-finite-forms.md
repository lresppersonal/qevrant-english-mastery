## overview | overview | Overview and purpose
Finite verbs show time and voice through tense and *be* + past participle: *He **was told**. She **has finished**.* But many structures need a **non-finite** form — an infinitive (*to do*) or an *-ing* form (*doing*) — after verbs, adjectives, prepositions and in reporting patterns. These forms can also carry **perfect** meaning (an earlier time) and **passive** meaning (the subject receives the action):
- *I'd like **to be consulted**.* (passive infinitive)
- *She seems **to have left**.* (perfect infinitive)
- *He hates **being interrupted**.* (passive -ing)
- ***Having been warned**, they took precautions.* (perfect passive -ing)
!rule Choose the non-finite form in two steps: **time** (same time → simple; earlier time → perfect) and **voice** (subject does it → active; subject receives it → passive).

## theory | theory | How it works: time and voice without tense
A non-finite verb has no tense of its own, so its time is understood **relative to the main verb**.
- A **simple** form shows the same time or a later time: *She seems **to know** the answer* (now). *I hope **to finish** soon* (later).
- A **perfect** form (*to have done, having done*) shows an **earlier** time, or completion before a point: *She seems **to have known** the answer all along* (earlier). *I hope **to have finished** by June* (completed before June).
- A **passive** form (*to be done, being done*) shows that the subject is affected by the action: *Nobody likes **being criticised** in public.*
- The **continuous** infinitive (*to be doing*) shows an action in progress: *He seems **to be working** late again.*
The forms combine, giving eight possibilities, though the perfect passive continuous is very rare.

## form | form | Formation: the full set
| | Infinitive | -ing form |
| simple active | *to ask* | *asking* |
| simple passive | *to be asked* | *being asked* |
| perfect active | *to have asked* | *having asked* |
| perfect passive | *to have been asked* | *having been asked* |
| continuous | *to be asking* | — |
| perfect continuous | *to have been asking* | — |
Negatives place *not* before the non-finite form: *I regret **not having asked**. She pretended **not to have heard**.*

## uses | uses | Main uses and contexts
#### 1. After seem, appear, happen, tend, claim, pretend
These verbs are followed by an infinitive whose time is relative to the main verb. Compare *He **seems to be** happy* (now) with *He **seems to have been** happy there* (earlier) and *He **seemed to have forgotten** us* (earlier than a past moment).
#### 2. Passive reporting structures
With *say, believe, think, know, report, expect, consider, estimate*, the pattern **subject + passive reporting verb + to-infinitive** is common in news and academic writing:
- *The painting **is believed to be** a forgery.* (belief now, about now)
- *The fire **is thought to have started** in the kitchen.* (belief now, about the past)
- *The company **is reported to be negotiating** a merger.* (in progress)
- *Two people **are known to have been injured**.* (past + passive)
#### 3. After verbs and prepositions that take -ing
After *avoid, deny, admit, remember, regret, resent, risk, mind*, and after any preposition, use *being done* for passive meaning and *having done* to stress an earlier action: *She **denied having taken** the documents. They **complained about being kept** waiting. He **resented not being invited**.* With *deny, admit, regret, remember*, the simple -ing often already implies an earlier time, so *having* is a choice for emphasis: *He admitted **taking** / **having taken** the money.*
#### 4. After adjectives and nouns
*It's a privilege **to have been chosen**. I was lucky **to be offered** the job. There's a risk of **being misunderstood**.*
#### 5. Perfect infinitive after modal-like and unreal expressions
Perfect infinitives express unreal or unfulfilled past events after *would like, would rather, would hate, was/were to, was supposed to, meant to*: *I **would like to have seen** her face* (but I didn't). *They **were to have married** in June* (but the wedding was cancelled).
#### 6. Perfect participle clauses
*Having + past participle* at the start of a sentence shows that one action was completed before another, and is typical of formal narrative and reports: ***Having reviewed** the evidence, the panel recommended no further action.* ***Having been rejected** twice, he finally won the grant.*

## examples | examples | Examples in context, explained
> ex: The bridge **is expected to be completed** next spring. || passive reporting + passive infinitive (future completion)
> ex: The ship **is thought to have sunk** in a storm in 1847. || present belief about a past event: perfect infinitive
> ex: She doesn't like **being told** what to do. || passive -ing after *like*
> ex: I'm sorry **to have kept** you waiting. || apology for something already done
> ex: **Having lived** abroad for years, she found the return difficult. || earlier action, perfect participle
> ex: The documents appear **to have been altered** after signing. || perfect passive: earlier, and the documents were affected
> ex: He claims **to be writing** a novel. || in progress at the time of claiming
> ex: I would rather **not have known**. || unreal past: I did know, and I wish I hadn't

## contrasts | contrasts | Comparison with related structures
| Pair | Difference |
| *He seems to work hard* vs *He seems to be working hard* | habit/general vs in progress now. |
| *She is said to live in Rome* vs *She is said to have lived in Rome* | current residence vs past residence. |
| *It is said that…* vs *X is said to…* | same meaning; the personal construction (*X is said to*) puts the topic first and is more compact. |
| *I remember telling him* vs *I remember being told* | I told him vs someone told me. |
| *After finishing* vs *Having finished* | both show sequence; *having finished* is more formal and works without a preposition. |
| *I'd like to see it* vs *I'd like to have seen it* | a present/future wish vs regret about the past. |

## register | register | Register and stylistic nuance
!register Passive reporting structures (*is believed to, is estimated to have*) and perfect participle clauses are hallmarks of formal writing: they make claims impersonal and compress two clauses into one. In conversation, people usually say *They think the fire started in the kitchen* or *After I'd read it, I…*.
Avoid stacking several non-finite forms in one sentence (*Having been asked to have been seen…*): this sounds heavy even in academic prose. Use a finite clause instead.

## errors | errors | Common mistakes advanced learners make
> x: He is said to be born in Vienna. || He is said **to have been born** in Vienna. (the birth was in the past)
> x: I hate to be interrupted when I'm working. || I hate **being interrupted** when I'm working. (both possible, but -ing is more natural for a general dislike)
> x: Having finished the report, the computer crashed. || **When I had** finished the report, the computer crashed. (dangling participle: the computer didn't finish the report)
> x: She denied to have taken the money. || She denied **taking / having taken** the money.
> x: The suspect is believed having left the country. || The suspect is believed **to have left** the country.
!warning A perfect participle clause must share the subject of the main clause. ***Having been repaired**, the engineers tested the engine* says that the engineers were repaired. Write: *Once the engine had been repaired, the engineers tested it.*

## advanced | advanced | Advanced applications
#### Hedged reporting in academic writing
Passive reporting with *appear, seem* and perfect infinitives lets writers report evidence cautiously: *The effect **appears to have been overestimated** in earlier studies.* Compare the stronger *Earlier studies overestimated the effect.*
#### Narrative compression
Literary and journalistic prose uses perfect and passive -ing clauses to move quickly: ***Having been** turned away by three publishers, the manuscript finally appeared in 1952, **to be praised** by critics and **ignored** by readers.* The final infinitive of result (*to be praised*) is a C2 stylistic device: it presents a later, often ironic, outcome.
#### Was to have + past participle
*was/were to have done* describes a planned event that did **not** happen: *The minister **was to have opened** the hospital, but she fell ill.* Compare *was to open* (planned; outcome unknown or it did happen).

## summary | summary | Summary
- Non-finite forms show time relative to the main verb: simple = same or later time, perfect = earlier time.
- Passive non-finite forms: *to be done, being done, to have been done, having been done*.
- Passive reporting: *X is said / believed / thought to do / to be doing / to have done*.
- *Having done* clauses show a completed earlier action and must share the main clause subject.
- *Would like / was supposed to / was to* + perfect infinitive = an unfulfilled past plan or wish.

+ I would like to have met your parents.

- I like to have met your parents.

> *Would like* + perfect infinitive describes an unfulfilled past wish.

### [error] @errors C1
Correct the sentence: *Having been delayed for six hours, the passengers' patience ran out.*
= Having been delayed for six hours, the passengers ran out of patience.
> The perfect participle clause must share its subject with the main clause: the passengers were delayed, not their patience.

### [gap] @uses C1
He strongly denied ___ (involve) in the scandal.
= being involved | having been involved
> Passive meaning after *deny*; the perfect form stresses that the involvement was earlier.

### [mc] @register C1
Which version is most typical of a formal news report?
+ Three people are reported to have been injured in the explosion.
- They are saying three people got hurt in the explosion.
- Three people, it is reported, they were injured in the explosion.
- Three people are reported being injured in the explosion.
> Passive reporting + perfect passive infinitive is compact and impersonal.

Rewrite with a perfect participle clause: *She had studied the data carefully, and then she rejected the hypothesis.*
= Having studied the data carefully, she rejected the hypothesis.
> *Having* + past participle shows the earlier, completed action; the subject is shared.

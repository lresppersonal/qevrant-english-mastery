## overview | overview | Definition and function
Register is the choice of language to suit reader, purpose and medium. Academic register is **precise, objective, cautious and dense**. Four grammatical tools create it: nominalisation, hedging, impersonal structures and formal vocabulary and linking.

## nominalisation | form | Nominalisation
Turning verbs and adjectives into nouns lets you treat processes as things and pack more information into a sentence.
| Verbal style | Nominal style |
| Prices increased quickly, which worried consumers. | **The rapid increase in prices** worried consumers. |
| We analysed the data and found that… | **Analysis of the data** revealed that… |
| Because people are using cars less, … | **The decline in car use** has… |
!warning Too much nominalisation becomes unreadable. Keep a clear subject and an active verb where possible.

## hedging | uses | Hedging: being appropriately cautious
| Tool | Examples |
| modal verbs | *may, might, could, would* |
| lexical verbs | *appear to, seem to, tend to, suggest, indicate* |
| adverbs | *possibly, arguably, apparently, largely, generally* |
| adjectives/nouns | *it is likely/possible that; there is evidence that* |
| quantifiers | *some, many, in most cases, to some extent* |
> ex: Social media **causes** depression. || too strong
> ex: Heavy social-media use **appears to be associated with** higher rates of depression **in some groups**. || hedged

## impersonal | uses | Impersonal and objective structures
- Passive: *The samples **were tested**.*
- Anticipatory *it*: ***It is widely accepted that** … **It could be argued that** …*
- *There* + be: ***There is** little evidence to support this view.*
- Abstract subjects: ***This study** examines… **The data** suggest…*
!register *I* and *we* are increasingly accepted in many disciplines, but IELTS-style essays usually avoid *I think*: prefer *In my view* once, or impersonal forms.

## informal | contrasts | Avoiding informal features
| Informal | Academic |
| a lot of, lots of | a great deal of, numerous, a large number of |
| get, got | obtain, become, receive |
| kids, stuff, things | children, materials, factors |
| don't, can't | do not, cannot |
| So, … / And … / But … (sentence start) | Therefore, … / In addition, … / However, … |
| phrasal verbs: *look into, cut down* | single verbs: *investigate, reduce* |
| rhetorical questions, exclamation marks | statements |

## errors | errors | Common mistakes
> x: Nowadays, there are a lot of people who think that technology is very bad for kids. || *Many people now argue that technology may harm children's development.*
> x: Scientists have proved that coffee is healthy. || Overclaiming: *Research suggests that moderate coffee consumption may have health benefits.*
> x: The government should solve this problem fast. || Vague: *Governments could address the problem by…*

## advanced | uses | Advanced usage and stylistic nuances
- **Boosters** for well-supported claims: *clearly, undoubtedly, demonstrate, it is evident that*. Balance boosters and hedges.
- **Evaluative stance markers**: *Surprisingly, Notably, Importantly*.
- **Cohesion through reference**: *this trend, such policies, these findings* (a summary noun after *this/these*).

## register | register | Register in IELTS-style essays
Aim for a formal-neutral tone: clear topic sentences, hedged claims, varied linking, no contractions or slang. Over-formality (*Henceforth, it is incumbent upon*) is as unnatural as informality. Clarity always comes first.

## summary | summary | Summary
- Nominalise to condense, but keep sentences readable.
- Hedge claims to match the strength of your evidence.
- Use impersonal structures and abstract subjects.
- Replace informal words, contractions and phrasal verbs.

### [transform] @nominalisation C1
Complete with 3–4 words. ‘Unemployment rose sharply, which led to protests.’ → ‘___ in unemployment led to protests.’
= The sharp rise | A sharp rise | The sharp increase | A sharp increase
> Nominalisation: adjective + noun replaces the clause.

### [mc] @hedging C1
Which sentence is most appropriately hedged?
+ The findings suggest that exercise may improve concentration in some students.
- The findings prove that exercise improves concentration.
- Exercise definitely improves concentration in every student.
- Exercise is very good for concentration.
> *suggest* + *may* + *some* qualify the claim to match the evidence.

### [mc] @informal C1
Choose the academic alternative to ‘look into the problem’.
+ investigate the problem
- check out the problem
- get into the problem
- go over to the problem
> Formal single verbs replace phrasal verbs.

### [mc] @impersonal C1
___ that urban growth will continue for several decades.
+ It is widely expected
- Everybody thinks
- I am totally sure
- People are saying
> Anticipatory *it* + passive is impersonal and formal.

### [error] @errors C1
Which part is too informal for an essay? *Governments should cut down on spending, but they can't ignore the needs of kids.*
+ cut down on / can't / kids
- Governments should
- spending
- the needs of
> Replace with *reduce*, *cannot*, *children*.

### [gap] @advanced C1
Several studies have reported higher stress levels among night workers. ___ findings highlight the need for better scheduling.
= These | Such
> Summary reference: *These / Such findings*.

### [rewrite] @hedging C1
Hedge the claim with *appear*: ‘Online learning reduces dropout rates.’ → ‘Online learning ___ dropout rates.’
= appears to reduce | would appear to reduce
> *appear to* + infinitive softens the claim.

### [mc] @nominalisation C2
Which version is clearest and most academic?
+ The introduction of congestion charges reduced traffic by 20%.
- When they introduced congestion charges, traffic went down by 20%.
- The reduction of traffic by 20% was the result of the introduction of the charging of congestion.
- Congestion charges got introduced and traffic was reduced 20%.
> Balanced nominalisation with an active verb; option C overuses nouns.

### [context] @register C2
Replace the informal phrase: ‘There are ___ (lots of) factors involved.’
= numerous | many | a number of | several | a range of
> Academic quantifiers replace *lots of*.

### [context] @impersonal C2
‘___ is little evidence to support the claim that bilingual children fall behind.’
= There
> *There is little evidence…* is an impersonal evaluative pattern.

## overview | overview | Overview and purpose
*All the students didn't pass* and *Not all the students passed* look similar but can mean different things. *I don't think it's fair* is more natural than *I think it isn't fair*. *Only* in different positions changes the claim. This lesson is about **what** a negative or focusing word applies to — its **scope** — and the conventions English uses to negate politely and precisely.

## theory | theory | How it works: scope and focus
A negative or focusing word affects the part of the sentence that follows it and falls within its **scope**. Compare:
> ex: She **deliberately didn't** answer. || deliberate act of not answering
> ex: She **didn't deliberately** answer. || she answered, but not deliberately (accidentally)
In speech, stress marks the focus: *I didn't say she **stole** the money* (I said something else about the money) vs *I didn't say **she** stole the money* (someone else did). In writing, there is no stress, so **word order** and **choice of structure** must make the scope clear.
!rule Put a negative or focusing word as close as possible to the element it applies to. When in doubt, restructure: *Not all of the data were usable* is clearer than *All of the data weren't usable*.

## form | form | Formation: the main patterns
#### Negative forms
| Form | Example | Note |
| not + verb | *The method does **not** work.* | clause negation |
| no + noun | *There is **no** evidence.* | stronger than *not any* |
| not any / not ... either | *We don't have **any** data. I didn't go **either**.* | neutral |
| none (of) | ***None of** the samples was / were contaminated.* | singular verb is formal |
| neither / nor | ***Neither** option is ideal. She didn't agree, **nor** did I.* | inversion after *nor* |
| never, nowhere, nobody, nothing | *I've **never** seen it.* | one negative per clause in standard English |
| hardly, scarcely, barely, seldom, rarely | *We **hardly** know her.* | negative meaning; take positive tags: *..., do we?* |
| little, few (no article) | *There is **little** hope. **Few** people agreed.* | negative: "not much/many" (*a little, a few* are positive) |
#### Focusing adverbs
| Adverb | Position and meaning |
| only | before the focused element in writing: *We **only** tested **adults*** (ambiguous) → *We tested **only adults*** (precise) |
| even | adds surprise: ***Even** experts disagree. They didn't **even** reply.* |
| just | = only/exactly: *It's **just** a theory. That's **just** what I meant.* |
| also / too / as well | addition: *also* mid-position; *too/as well* end position |
| especially, particularly, mainly | restrict to a subset: *The effect was strong, **particularly among** older users.* |

## uses | uses | Main uses
#### 1. Negative transfer (raising)
With *think, believe, suppose, expect, imagine, seem, appear*, English normally negates the main verb, not the clause: *I **don't think** he's right* (not *I think he isn't right*), *It **doesn't seem** likely.* This sounds less direct and more polite.
#### 2. Partial versus total negation
***Not all** respondents agreed* = some did not. ***No** respondents agreed / **None** of the respondents agreed* = zero agreement. *All respondents didn't agree* is ambiguous; avoid it in writing.
#### 3. Understatement (litotes)
Negating a negative adjective gives a cautious positive: *not uncommon* (fairly common), *not unreasonable*, *not insignificant*, *not without problems*. Very common in academic and diplomatic English.
#### 4. Negative tags and responses
After negative adverbs, use positive tags: *She **seldom** complains, **does** she?* Responses agree with the negative: *"I can't swim." "**Neither** can I." / "Nor can I." / "Me neither."*

## examples | examples | Examples in context, explained
> ex: I **don't believe** the figures have been checked. || negative transfer: tactful doubt
> ex: **Not every** policy that sounds fair is fair in practice. || partial negation
> ex: There is **little** evidence to support this claim. || *little* = almost none, critical tone
> ex: The results are **not insignificant**. || litotes: cautious way to say *quite significant*
> ex: **Only** later did the team realise the error. || fronted *only* + inversion for emphasis
> ex: The change affected **only** part-time staff. || *only* placed directly before the focus
> ex: He **didn't even** apologise. || *even* stresses that the minimum expected action was missing

## contrasts | contrasts | Comparison with related structures
| Pair | Difference |
| *little* / *a little* | negative (*little time* = not enough) vs positive (*a little time* = some). Same for *few / a few*. |
| *no* / *not* | *no* + noun is more emphatic: *It's **no** surprise* vs *It's **not a** surprise*. *Not* before adjectives: *not easy*. |
| *any* / *no* after negatives | *I didn't see anyone* = *I saw no one* (formal). Never *~~I didn't see no one~~* in standard English. |
| *hardly* / *hard* | *hardly* = almost not; *hard* = with effort. *Hardly any/ever* = almost none/never. |
| *also* / *too* / *either* | positive addition *also/too*; negative addition *either*: *I don't like it **either*** (~~too~~). |

## register | register | Register and stylistic nuance
!register Formal: *no, none, neither, nor, scarcely, seldom, little, few*. Neutral/informal: *not any, not ... either, hardly, me neither*. Double negatives (*I don't know nothing*) are features of some dialects and informal speech, but non-standard in writing.
Litotes and negative transfer are key politeness devices in British English: *That's **not the best** idea* often means *That's a bad idea*. Be aware of this when interpreting feedback.

## errors | errors | Common mistakes advanced learners make
> x: I think it isn't a good idea. || I don't think it's a good idea.
> x: All the students didn't pass the exam. (meaning some failed) || Not all the students passed the exam.
> x: I don't like coffee too. || I don't like coffee either.
> x: He hardly didn't sleep. || He hardly slept.
> x: Few people came, so it was crowded. || Few people came, so it was quiet. / A few people came ... (meaning changes)
> x: None of us have no idea. || None of us has any idea. / None of us have any idea.

## advanced | advanced | Advanced applications
#### Negative inversion for rhetoric
***Under no circumstances** should the data be shared. **Not only** was it late, it was incomplete. **At no point** did the minister apologise.* (See the inversion lesson.)
#### Scope in academic claims
*This does **not necessarily** mean ...* / *This **necessarily does not** mean ...*: the first denies necessity (it may or may not mean it); the second asserts a necessary negative. Precision here matters in argument.
#### Focusing adverbs with numbers
*The scheme reached **just** 12% of eligible households* (= a disappointingly small number) vs *as many as 12%* (= surprisingly large). The adverb carries the writer's evaluation.

## summary | summary | Summary
- Place *not, only, even* close to what they affect; restructure to avoid ambiguous scope.
- Prefer negative transfer: *I don't think X* rather than *I think not-X*.
- *Not all* = partial; *no/none* = total.
- *Little/few* are negative, *a little/a few* positive.
- Use litotes (*not uncommon*) for cautious claims; use positive tags after *hardly, seldom*.

## core | core | Core concept
Multi-word verbs combine a verb with one or two particles: *give up, look into, put up with*. Their meaning is often not the sum of the parts, and their **grammar depends on the type**: whether the particle is an adverb (separable) or a preposition (not separable).
!rule Separable phrasal verbs: the object can go before or after the particle, but a pronoun must go in the middle: *turn **it** off*. Prepositional verbs: the object always follows: *look **after** her*.
> ex: Turn off the light. / Turn the light off. / Turn **it** off. || separable: a pronoun goes in the middle
> ex: She looks after her grandmother. / She looks after **her**. || prepositional: never *looks her after*
> ex: I can't put up with this noise. || three-part verb: never separated

## overview | overview | Overview and purpose
English has thousands of verbs made of a verb plus a particle: *give up, look into, put up with, carry out*. They are not one category but three, and their grammar differs: where the object can go, whether the particle is stressed, and whether they can be passivised. Advanced users also need to know when a phrasal verb is the natural choice and when a formal equivalent is better.

## theory | theory | How it works: adverb particles and prepositions
The particle after the verb is either an **adverb** (*up, out, away, back, down, off*) or a **preposition** (*at, for, into, with, on*).
- **Phrasal verbs** (verb + adverb particle): the particle belongs to the verb and changes its meaning: *give up* (stop), *turn down* (refuse). The particle can usually move: *turn down **the offer** / turn **the offer** down*.
- **Prepositional verbs** (verb + preposition): the preposition belongs to the object noun phrase: *look **at the chart**, rely **on volunteers***. The object always follows the preposition.
- **Phrasal-prepositional verbs** (verb + adverb + preposition): *put up **with**, look forward **to**, come up **with**, get away **with***. The object follows the whole sequence.
!rule A pronoun object goes **between** the verb and the adverb particle of a phrasal verb: *turn **it** down* (~~turn down it~~), but **after** the preposition of a prepositional verb: *look **at it***.

## form | form | Formation: the main patterns
| Type | Object position | Example | Passive |
| intransitive phrasal | no object | *The plane **took off**.* | none |
| transitive phrasal (separable) | before or after particle; pronoun before | *She **set up** the company / **set** it **up**.* | *The company **was set up** in 2010.* |
| transitive phrasal (inseparable, fixed) | after particle | *They **looked after** the children.* (prepositional in fact) | *The children **were looked after**.* |
| prepositional | after preposition | *We **depend on** donations.* | *Donations **are** heavily **depended on**.* (possible) |
| phrasal-prepositional | after the last particle | *I won't **put up with** this.* | *This **won't be put up with**.* (informal) |
| verb + object + particle only | object must separate | *The noise **kept** me **up**.* | *I **was kept up** by the noise.* |
#### Stress
Adverb particles are usually stressed: *She turned it **DOWN**.* Prepositions are usually unstressed: *She **LOOKED** at it.*
#### Long objects
With a long object, put the particle first: *They turned **down** every proposal that the committee had submitted* (not *turned every proposal ... down*).

## uses | uses | Main uses
#### 1. Literal, aspectual and idiomatic meaning
- **Literal**: *take the boxes **out***.
- **Aspectual** (the particle adds completion, continuation or beginning): *eat **up**, use **up*** (completely); *carry **on**, keep **on*** (continue); *set **off**, start **up*** (begin).
- **Idiomatic**: *put off* (postpone), *come across* (find by chance), *bring about* (cause).
#### 2. Nouns and adjectives from phrasal verbs
*break down → a breakdown; turn out → the turnout; set back → a setback; cut back → cutbacks; drop out → a dropout; outcome, outbreak, input, upkeep, follow-up, built-up area, worn-out shoes.* These nouns are common even in formal writing.
#### 3. Register choice
Many phrasal verbs have a Latinate equivalent: *find out / discover, put off / postpone, set up / establish, look into / investigate, carry out / conduct, go up / increase, point out / indicate.* Phrasal verbs are natural in speech and most journalism; academic writing often prefers the single verb, but some phrasal verbs are fully academic: *carry out, point out, set out, account for, rule out, draw on, bring about*.

## examples | examples | Examples in context, explained
> ex: The researchers **carried out** a series of interviews. || academic phrasal verb, natural in formal writing
> ex: We need to **rule out** other explanations before drawing conclusions. || *rule out* = exclude as a possibility
> ex: I **came across** an interesting article; I'll send it **over**. || idiomatic + literal-directional
> ex: The proposal was **turned down** because it was too expensive. || passive of a separable phrasal verb
> ex: You shouldn't **put up with** that kind of behaviour. || phrasal-prepositional, object at the end
> ex: The strike led to a major **setback** for the negotiations. || noun from *set back*
> ex: The decline **can be accounted for** by changes in demand. || passive prepositional verb in academic style

## contrasts | contrasts | Comparison with related structures
| Pair | Difference |
| *look up a word* vs *look up the street* | phrasal (search for: *look it up*) vs verb + prepositional phrase (direction: *look up it* is impossible; *look up the street* = towards). |
| *call on* vs *call up* | prepositional (visit / formally ask: *call on someone*) vs phrasal (phone; summon: *call someone up*). |
| *turn down* vs *refuse* | same meaning; *turn down* is neutral/informal, *refuse/decline* more formal. |
| *get over* vs *overcome* | *get over* an illness or disappointment (recover); *overcome* a difficulty (formal, effort). |
| *make up* (several meanings) | invent (*make up a story*), constitute (*women make up 60%*), reconcile (*they made up*), compensate (*make up for lost time*). Context decides. |

## register | register | Register and stylistic nuance
!register Informal phrasal verbs such as *get on with, mess up, sort out, chuck out* belong in speech and informal writing. In essays, prefer *proceed, spoil, resolve, discard* — but do not replace academic phrasal verbs like *carry out, point out, account for* with odd formal equivalents.
Overly formal single verbs in conversation sound stiff: *We must postpone the meeting* is fine at work, but among friends *Let's put it off* is more natural.

## errors | errors | Common mistakes
!! I looked up it in the dictionary. || I looked it up in the dictionary. || With a separable phrasal verb, a pronoun object goes between the verb and the particle.
!! Can you pick up me at eight? || Can you pick me up at eight? || Pronoun object in the middle.
!! They called off it because of the rain. || They called it off because of the rain. || Pronoun object in the middle.
!! She is looking forward to meet you. || She is looking forward to meeting you. || *To* is a preposition, so it is followed by *-ing*.
!! I'll look her after this weekend. || I'll look after her this weekend. || *Look after* is a prepositional verb: the object always follows the preposition.
## advanced | advanced | Advanced applications
#### Particles with consistent meanings
Learning particle meanings speeds up acquisition: **up** = completion or increase (*fill up, step up, scale up*); **down** = reduction or recording (*scale down, write down, narrow down*); **out** = to the end or outward (*sort out, wear out, phase out*); **off** = separation or cancellation (*call off, cut off, write off*); **through** = to completion (*follow through, see through*).
#### Academic phrasal verbs worth mastering
*account for, bear out, build on, carry out, draw on, embark on, factor in, point out, rule out, set out, single out, spell out, stem from, weigh up.*
#### Particle placement for emphasis
In speech the object can follow the particle for end-focus even if short: *He gave back **the money** — all of it.* But with pronouns, separation remains obligatory.

## summary | summary | Summary
- Phrasal verbs: verb + adverb particle; object can separate; pronouns go in the middle.
- Prepositional verbs: verb + preposition; object always after the preposition.
- Phrasal-prepositional verbs: object after both particles (*put up with it*).
- Many phrasal verbs form nouns (*setback, outcome, breakdown*).
- Choose register carefully: some phrasal verbs are academic, others are informal.

## core | core | Core concept
Punctuation is not decoration: it **shows the grammar of a sentence** to the reader. A full stop ends a complete idea; a comma separates parts *inside* a sentence; a semicolon joins two complete, closely related ideas; a colon announces that what follows explains or lists what came before.
!rule Two complete sentences cannot be joined with a comma alone. Use a full stop, a semicolon, or a comma + *and / but / or / so / yet*.
> ex: Prices rose sharply**;** demand, however, remained stable. || two complete ideas, closely linked
> ex: The study had one major limitation**:** the sample was small. || the colon introduces an explanation
> ex: Although the results were promising**,** further research is needed. || comma after a fronted subordinate clause

## uses | uses | When and why we use each mark
- **Comma**: after an introductory clause or phrase (*In 2020, …*); before *and/but/so* joining two full clauses; around extra (non-essential) information; between items in a list.
- **Semicolon**: between two complete clauses that are closely related; before a sentence adverb such as *however* or *therefore* when it joins two clauses; to separate long list items that already contain commas.
- **Colon**: after a complete clause, to introduce a list, an explanation, an example or a quotation.
- **Apostrophe**: possession (*the government's plan*, *the students' results*) and contractions (*it's = it is*). Never for plurals.
- **Dash**: in less formal writing, to add emphasis or an aside. In formal writing, commas or brackets are usually safer.
- **Hyphen**: to join words that work together before a noun (*a well-known study*, *long-term effects*).

## form | form | How the patterns are built
| Pattern | Punctuation | Example |
| Main clause + main clause | ; or . or , + and/but/so | *Costs fell; profits rose.* |
| Main clause + *however* + main clause | ; however, | *Costs fell; however, profits did not rise.* |
| Subordinate clause first | clause**,** main clause | *If costs fall, profits rise.* |
| Main clause first | main clause + subordinate clause (no comma) | *Profits rise if costs fall.* |
| Non-defining relative clause | **,** who/which … **,** | *The report, which was published in May, …* |
| Defining relative clause | no commas | *Students who submit late lose marks.* |
| Introducing a list | complete clause**:** item, item and item | *Three factors matter: cost, speed and safety.* |
!tip Read the part before a colon on its own. If it is not a complete sentence (*The factors are:*), remove the colon.

## examples | examples | Examples in context
> ex: In most developed countries**,** life expectancy has risen steadily. || comma after an introductory phrase
> ex: The data were collected in 2019**,** and the analysis was completed a year later. || comma + *and* between two full clauses
> ex: Remote work offers flexibility**;** however**,** it can blur the line between work and home. || semicolon + *however* + comma
> ex: The council**,** which had promised new housing**,** delayed the project again. || commas around a non-defining clause
> ex: Researchers identified two risks**:** rising costs and falling participation. || colon before a list after a complete clause
> ex: The **company's** profits fell, but its **employees'** salaries rose. || singular and plural possession

## contrasts | contrasts | Commonly confused choices
> ex: its / it's || *its* = belonging to it (*the city and its history*); *it's* = it is / it has
> ex: however, / , however, || *However, costs rose.* (start of a sentence) vs *Costs, however, rose.* (inside a clause) vs *Prices fell; however, costs rose.* (joining two clauses: semicolon first)
> ex: which (with commas) / that (without) || *The report, which…* adds extra information; *the report that…* identifies which report
> ex: colon / semicolon || a colon introduces something; a semicolon joins two equal clauses
> ex: hyphen / dash || a hyphen joins words (*long-term*); a dash separates parts of a sentence

## errors | errors | Common mistakes
!! The results were clear, the hypothesis was confirmed. || The results were clear; the hypothesis was confirmed. || This is a comma splice: two complete sentences need a full stop, a semicolon or a conjunction.
!! Costs fell, however profits did not increase. || Costs fell; however, profits did not increase. || *However* is an adverb, not a conjunction. Use a semicolon before it and a comma after it.
!! The main factors are: cost, time and quality. || The main factors are cost, time and quality. || A colon must follow a complete clause. *The main factors are* is not complete.
!! Students, who submit late, lose marks. || Students who submit late lose marks. || This is a defining clause (only the late students lose marks), so it takes no commas.
!! The governments policy was criticised. || The government's policy was criticised. || Possession needs an apostrophe.
!! In the 1990's many factory's closed. || In the 1990s many factories closed. || Plurals and decades do not take apostrophes.
!! Unemployment fell which surprised economists. || Unemployment fell, which surprised economists. || A *which* clause that comments on the whole previous clause needs a comma.

## register | register | Formal writing and nuance
Academic writing prefers full stops, commas, semicolons and colons; dashes, exclamation marks and contractions (*don't, it's*) are usually avoided. Long sentences are acceptable if the punctuation makes their structure clear, but a sequence of several commas often signals that a sentence should be split.
!tip At C1–C2, a semicolon can balance two contrasting ideas elegantly: *Some regions prospered; others were left behind.* Use it sparingly: one or two in a paragraph is enough.
> ex: The policy had three aims**:** to cut costs**;** to improve access**,** especially in rural areas**;** and to raise standards. || semicolons separate list items that already contain commas

## summary | summary | Summary
- Never join two complete sentences with a comma alone.
- *however / therefore* between clauses: **; however,**
- Commas around non-defining clauses; none around defining clauses.
- A colon follows a complete clause.
- Apostrophes for possession and contractions, never for plurals.

### [mc] @errors B2
Which sentence is correctly punctuated?
+ The museum was closed; we visited the park instead.
- The museum was closed, we visited the park instead.
- The museum was closed we visited the park instead.
- The museum, was closed; we visited the park instead.
> Two complete sentences: use a semicolon or full stop, not a comma alone (comma splice).

### [mc] @uses B2
Choose the correct punctuation: ‘Sales increased in March ___ they fell again in April.’
+ , but
- ; but,
- : but
- , however
> A comma + coordinating conjunction (*but*) can join two full clauses. *However* would need a semicolon before it.

### [mc] @contrasts B2
Prices rose sharply ___ demand remained stable.
+ ; however,
- , however
- however,
- , however;
> *However* joining two clauses: semicolon before, comma after.

### [mc] @form B2
Which sentence needs NO comma?
+ Students who miss the deadline will lose marks.
- Although it was late, the meeting continued.
- The report, which was published in May, was widely read.
- In 2021, the policy was changed.
> A defining relative clause identifies the noun, so it takes no commas.

### [mc] @uses B2+
Which use of the colon is correct?
+ The plan has one weakness: it is too expensive.
- The reasons for the decision were: cost and time.
- We need to buy: paper, pens and folders.
- She said: that she would come later.
> A colon must follow a complete clause and introduce an explanation, list or quotation.

### [gap] @errors B2
Write the correct form: ‘The ___ (company) profits fell, but its employees kept their jobs.’
= company's
> Singular possession: *company's*.

### [gap] @errors B2+
Write the correct form: ‘All the ___ (student) results were published online.’
= students'
> Plural noun ending in -s + apostrophe after the s.

### [mc] @contrasts B2
The city is proud of ___ long history.
+ its
- it's
- its'
- it is
> *its* = belonging to it; *it's* = it is.

### [error] @errors B2+
Which part is incorrect? *Unemployment fell sharply last year which surprised many economists.*
+ last year which
- Unemployment fell
- sharply
- many economists
> A sentence-relative *which* clause needs a comma: *…last year, which surprised…*

### [error] @errors B2+
Which part is incorrect? *The three main causes are: poverty, poor housing and lack of education.*
+ are:
- The three main causes
- poor housing
- lack of education
> A colon must follow a complete clause; remove it after *are*.

### [mc] @form B2+
Choose the correctly punctuated sentence.
+ If governments invest in public transport, car use tends to fall.
- If governments invest in public transport car use, tends to fall.
- If governments, invest in public transport car use tends to fall.
- If governments invest, in public transport, car use tends to fall.
> Fronted subordinate clause → comma before the main clause.

### [mc] @examples C1
Which sentence uses commas to show that ALL the scientists were consulted?
+ The scientists, who were consulted in advance, supported the plan.
- The scientists who were consulted in advance supported the plan.
- The scientists who, were consulted in advance supported the plan.
- The scientists who were consulted, in advance supported the plan.
> Commas make the clause non-defining: it adds information about all the scientists.

### [transform] @uses C1
Join with a semicolon and *therefore*. ‘The sample was small. The results should be treated with caution.’ → ‘The sample was small___ the results should be treated with caution.’
= ; therefore,
> Sentence adverb between two clauses: semicolon before, comma after.

### [mc] @register C1
Which sentence is most appropriate in formal academic writing?
+ The findings were unexpected; nevertheless, they were consistent with earlier studies.
- The findings were unexpected – but they matched earlier studies!
- The findings weren't expected, but they matched what others found.
- The findings were unexpected, nevertheless they were consistent with earlier studies.
> Formal style avoids dashes, exclamation marks and contractions; *nevertheless* between clauses needs a semicolon.

### [mc] @register C2
Which sentence punctuates a list of complex items correctly?
+ The strategy had three aims: to reduce costs; to improve access, particularly in rural areas; and to raise standards.
- The strategy had three aims: to reduce costs, to improve access, particularly in rural areas, and to raise standards.
- The strategy had three aims; to reduce costs, to improve access, particularly in rural areas, and to raise standards.
- The strategy had three aims, to reduce costs; to improve access; particularly in rural areas; and to raise standards.
> When list items contain commas, semicolons separate the items so the reader can see where each one ends.

### [context] @uses C1
‘The results were striking___ participation doubled within a year.’ (the second part explains the first)
= :
> A colon introduces an explanation of the complete clause before it.

=== recognise
@title A report on cycling in cities
+ Over the past decade[[,]] cycling has grown in many European cities. || comma after an introductory phrase
+ The growth has been uneven[[;]] some cities have doubled their numbers, while others have seen little change. || semicolon joining two closely related complete clauses
- Most surveys ask people how often they cycle and why.
+ Two factors stand out[[:]] safe lanes and short distances. || colon introducing a list after a complete clause
+ Amsterdam[[,]] which has invested heavily for decades[[,]] remains the best-known example. || commas around a non-defining relative clause
- Cyclists who feel safe are more likely to cycle every day.
+ Costs are relatively low[[; however,]] political support can be difficult to secure. || semicolon + *however* + comma between two clauses

=== check
### [mc] Which sentence contains a comma splice?
+ The data were incomplete, the conclusions were uncertain.
- The data were incomplete, so the conclusions were uncertain.
- Because the data were incomplete, the conclusions were uncertain.
> Two full sentences joined by a comma alone form a comma splice.
### [mc] Where does the comma go? ‘Profits fell however the company kept all its staff.’
- Profits fell, however the company kept all its staff.
+ Profits fell; however, the company kept all its staff.
- Profits fell however, the company kept all its staff.
> *However* between two clauses: semicolon before, comma after.
### [mc] Which needs an apostrophe?
+ the governments decision
- the 1990s
- three reports
> Possession: *the government's decision*. Decades and plurals take no apostrophe.
### [explain] Why do these two sentences mean different things? *The players, who were injured, did not travel.* / *The players who were injured did not travel.*
= With commas, all the players were injured and none travelled; without commas, only the injured players stayed behind.
> Commas make a relative clause non-defining; without them, the clause identifies which players.

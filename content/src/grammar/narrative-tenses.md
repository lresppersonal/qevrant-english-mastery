## overview | overview | Definition and function
Narrative tenses organise a story in time. The **past simple** moves the story forward; the **past continuous** describes the background; the **past perfect** steps back to an earlier event; the **past perfect continuous** shows an activity that continued up to a past moment.
> ex: It **was raining** and the streets **were** empty. I **had been walking** for an hour when I **realised** I **had left** my keys at the office.

## form | form | Formation
| Tense | Form | Function in a story |
| Past simple | V2 (*went, decided*) | main events in sequence |
| Past continuous | was/were + -ing | background, interrupted actions |
| Past perfect | had + V3 | events before the main time |
| Past perfect continuous | had been + -ing | duration up to a past moment, causes |

## uses | uses | Main rules and usage
- Use the **past perfect** only when you need to make the earlier time clear. If events are told in order, the past simple is enough: *I got up, had a shower and left.*
- Use it after **time conjunctions** when the order matters: *When I arrived, the film started* (I arrived, then it started) vs *When I arrived, the film had started* (it started before).
- With **before, after, as soon as**, the past perfect is optional because the conjunction already shows the order.
- Use the **past perfect continuous** to explain a visible result in the past: *Her eyes were red: she had been crying.*

## time | time | Time expressions in narratives
!rule Linkers show the order of events; the tense shows how the speaker sees them. Choose the linker first, then check the tense.
| Expression | Typical tense | Example |
| **when, as soon as, the moment** | past simple (sequence) | **When** the alarm rang, we **left**. |
| **while, as, just as** | past continuous (background) | **While** we **were packing**, the lights went out. |
| **by the time, before, already, (not) yet** | past perfect (earlier event) | **By the time** we arrived, the film **had started**. |
| **for, since** (looking back from a past point) | past perfect continuous | She **had been working** there **for** years. |
| **after, once** | past simple or past perfect | **After** he **(had) finished**, he went home. |
| **then, next, eventually, in the end, at last** | past simple | **Eventually**, the rain **stopped**. |
> ex: **No sooner had** we sat down **than** the phone rang. || Inverted pattern for emphasis — see *Inversion and emphasis*.
> ex: **Hardly** had she arrived **when** the trouble began. || *hardly/scarcely … when*
!tip *After* and *before* already show order, so the past perfect is optional: *After she (had) left, I called him.*

## contrasts | contrasts | Differences between similar constructions
> ex: When she came in, we **had** dinner. || She came in, then we ate.
> ex: When she came in, we **were having** dinner. || We were in the middle of dinner.
> ex: When she came in, we **had had** dinner. || We had already finished.
> ex: I **had read** the book twice. || completed number of times
> ex: I **had been reading** for hours. || duration of the activity

## variations | uses | Other ways to talk about the past
- **used to / would** for past habits (see *Habits: used to and would*).
- **was going to / was about to** for plans that did not happen: *I was going to call you, but I forgot.*
- **The historic present**: storytellers and journalists sometimes use present tenses to make a past story vivid: *So I'm walking home, and this man stops me…* (informal speech, C1).

## errors | errors | Common mistakes
> x: After I had finished, I had gone home. || Only the earlier action needs the past perfect: *After I had finished, I went home.*
> x: I was knowing the answer. || State verb: *I knew the answer.*
> x: Yesterday I had met an old friend. || No earlier past reference point: *Yesterday I met an old friend.*

## advanced | advanced | Advanced usage and stylistic nuances
- Writers use the past perfect for **flashbacks**, then return to the past simple once the earlier time is established, to avoid a long chain of *had*.
- **Hardly / No sooner** with the past perfect and inversion (formal): *No sooner had we sat down than the alarm went off.* (See *Inversion and emphasis*.)
- **Perfect infinitives** for reported past: *He is said to have left the country.*

## register | register | Formal and academic use
In historical and academic narratives, the past perfect frames background before the main period: *By 1900, the city's population had doubled.* Avoid the historic present in formal essays unless you are summarising a literary plot, where the present simple is standard: *In the novel, the protagonist returns home…*

## summary | summary | Summary
- Past simple moves the story; continuous gives background; perfect steps back.
- Use the past perfect only where order would otherwise be unclear.
- Past perfect continuous explains causes and duration up to a past point.

=== practice
### [mc] @uses B2
By the time the ambulance arrived, the man ___.
+ had stopped breathing
- stopped breathing
- was stopping breathing
- has stopped breathing
> *By the time* + past → the earlier event takes the past perfect.

### [gap] @contrasts B2
I ___ (walk) home when it started to pour with rain.
= was walking
> An action in progress interrupted by another → past continuous.

### [gap] @uses B2+
She looked exhausted because she ___ (work) all night.
= had been working | 'd been working
> Cause of a visible past result, with duration → past perfect continuous.

### [mc] @contrasts B2+
When we got to the cinema, the film ___, so we missed the beginning.
+ had already started
- already started
- was starting
- has already started
> The film started before we arrived → past perfect.

### [error] @errors B2+
Which part is incorrect? *After the guests had left, we had cleaned the whole flat.*
+ we had cleaned
- After the guests
- had left
- the whole flat
> Only the earlier event needs the past perfect: *we cleaned*.

### [mc] @variations B2+
I ___ call you last night, but my phone died.
+ was going to
- would
- had been going
- used to
> A plan that did not happen → *was going to*.

### [transform] @advanced C1
Complete with 4 words. ‘As soon as we sat down, the alarm went off.’ → ‘No sooner ___ than the alarm went off.’
= had we sat down
> *No sooner* at the start triggers inversion with the past perfect.

### [mc] @register C1
Choose the best sentence for a history essay.
+ By the end of the decade, the industry had collapsed.
- By the end of the decade, the industry collapses.
- By the end of the decade, the industry has collapsed.
- By the end of the decade, the industry was collapsing already.
> *By* + past time → past perfect for completion before that point.

### [rewrite] @advanced C1
Rewrite with a perfect infinitive: ‘People say that he left the country.’ → ‘He is said ___ the country.’
= to have left
> A past event after a present reporting verb → *to have* + past participle.

### [context] @advanced C2
‘Hardly ___ the door when the phone rang.’ (I / close)
= had I closed
> *Hardly … when* takes inversion and the past perfect.

## overview | overview | Why be vague?
Being vague is not bad English. We use **vague language** when exact detail isn't important, to sound friendly, or because we don't know. **Fillers** give us time to think without long silences.
> ex: There were **about thirty or so** people there.
> ex: We need pens, paper, **that kind of thing**.
> ex: **Well**, **I mean**, it's **sort of** complicated.

## form | form | Types of expression
| Function | Expressions |
| approximate numbers | **around / about / roughly** 50, 50 **or so**, 50**-ish**, **just over / under** 50, **dozens of** |
| approximate time | **sixish**, **at some point**, **the other day**, **sometime** next week |
| vague categories | **and stuff (like that)**, **and things**, **or something**, **that sort of thing**, **and so on** |
| softening adjectives | **sort of / kind of** tired, **a bit** expensive, **slightly** odd, **quite** good |
| fillers & thinking time | **well, um, er, you know, I mean, like, let me think, how can I put it** |
| vague nouns | **thing, stuff, whatsit, thingy** (very informal) |

## uses | uses | Using them well
- **Softening** criticism or requests: *It's **a bit** small, isn't it?* / *Could you **possibly** move your bag?*
- **Hedging** opinions: *I **tend to** think… / It **seems to me** that… / I'd **probably** say…*
- **Gaining time** in speaking tasks: *That's an interesting question. **Let me think**… / **Well**, it depends really.*
- **Reformulating**: *She's ambitious — **I mean**, she really wants to succeed.* / ***Or rather**, she was.*
!tip In speaking exams, one or two fillers are natural; constant *um* and *like* reduce your fluency score. Use "useful" fillers that also organise your answer: *Well, there are two main reasons…*

## contrasts | contrasts | Spoken vagueness vs academic hedging
| Speech (informal) | Academic writing (formal) |
| It's **kind of** risky. | It **may be** risky / This **appears to** carry some risk. |
| **Loads of** people use it. | **A large proportion of** users… |
| **About** half. | **Approximately** half / **roughly** 50%. |
| …**and stuff like that**. | …**and similar** factors / **among others**. |
| **I reckon** it'll fail. | It **seems likely** that it will fail. |
!register *Stuff, things, kind of, like, loads of* never appear in essays. Use hedging (*may, tend to, suggest, appear*) instead.

## errors | errors | Common mistakes
> x: There were about 30 or so of approximately people. || Use one vagueness marker: *about 30* **or** *30 or so*.
> x: She's a kind of tired. || *kind of* + adjective has no article: *She's **kind of** tired.*
> x: In conclusion, the government should do something and stuff. || Too informal for writing: *…and take similar measures.*
> x: Like, um, like, I like, um, think… || Overuse of fillers damages fluency. Pause briefly instead.

## advanced | advanced | Fine-tuning tone
> ex: It's **not exactly** cheap. || understatement = it's expensive
> ex: He's **not the most** reliable person. || polite criticism
> ex: I **wouldn't say** it was a disaster, but… || distancing
> ex: It was, **if anything**, a little too long. || cautious opinion
> ex: **For what it's worth**, I think you made the right choice. || modest opinion
!tip Understatement and *not exactly / not the most* are very common in British English and can sound more critical than they look.

## summary | summary | Summary
!rule Vague language is natural and polite in speech; in academic writing, replace it with formal hedging.
- Numbers: *around, roughly, or so, -ish*
- Categories: *and that kind of thing, or something*
- Softeners: *a bit, sort of, slightly*; fillers: *well, I mean, let me think*

=== practice
### [mc] @form B2
The meeting should finish at six-___.
+ ish
- like
- or so on
- sort
> *-ish* after a time/number = approximately.

### [mc] @uses B2
(Polite criticism) "The flat is ___ small for four people, isn't it?"
- very
+ a bit
- extremely
- totally
> *a bit* softens criticism.

### [gap] @form B2
We bought plates, cups, napkins, that ___ of thing.
= kind|sort
> *that kind/sort of thing* = similar items.

### [error] @errors B2
Correct the mistake: "I'm a kind of nervous about the interview."
= I'm kind of nervous about the interview.
> No article before *kind of* + adjective.

### [mc] @contrasts B2+
Which is most suitable for an essay?
- Loads of students use AI tools.
- Students use AI tools and stuff.
+ A large proportion of students use AI tools.
- Like, most students use AI tools.
> Formal writing avoids *loads of, and stuff, like*.

### [transform] @contrasts C1
Rewrite formally: "I reckon the plan will kind of fail."
= It seems likely that the plan will fail.|The plan seems likely to fail.|The plan is likely to fail.|It appears likely that the plan will fail.
> Replace spoken vagueness with formal hedging.

### [mc] @advanced C1
"How was the hotel?" "Well, it was ___ luxurious." (= quite basic)
+ not exactly
- not at all exactly
- exactly not
- not so exactly
> *not exactly* + adjective is an understatement.

### [context] @uses B2+
(In a speaking exam, to gain time) "___ — I'd probably say the main reason is cost."
- Like, um, like
+ That's an interesting question, let me think
- Stuff and things
- Or something
> A useful filler that also signals you are about to answer.

### [gap] @form B2+
The repairs will cost ___ £300 — I can't give you an exact figure yet.
= around|about|roughly|approximately
> *around / about / roughly* make a number approximate.

### [error] @contrasts C1
Correct for an academic essay: "Loads of people think online learning is kind of useless."
= Many people believe that online learning is of limited value.|Many people consider online learning to be of limited value.|A large number of people believe online learning is ineffective.|Many people think online learning is of little use.
> Replace *loads of* and *kind of* with formal quantifiers and hedging.

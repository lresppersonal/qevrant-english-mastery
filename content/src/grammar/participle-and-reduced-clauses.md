## core | core | Core concept
A participle clause packs a whole clause into a few words, without a subject or a full verb. It lets you link ideas (time, reason, result) or describe a noun concisely, which is why it is typical of good written English.
!rule *-ing* = active (the subject does it) · past participle = passive (the subject receives it) · *having* + past participle = it happened earlier. The subject of the participle must be the subject of the main clause.
> ex: **Feeling tired**, she went to bed early. || = Because she felt tired
> ex: The man **sitting by the window** is my tutor. || = who is sitting
> ex: **Written in 1850**, the novel still feels modern. || passive: the novel was written

## overview | overview | Definition and function
A participle clause is a clause without a subject and without a full verb. It is built on a **present participle** (*-ing*), a **past participle** (*-ed / V3*) or a **perfect participle** (*having + V3*). It can describe time, reason, result, condition or add information about a noun.
> ex: **Feeling tired**, she went to bed early. || = Because she felt tired
> ex: The man **sitting by the window** is my tutor. || = who is sitting

## form | form | Formation
| Form | Meaning | Example |
| -ing | active, same time or reason | ***Walking** home, I met Ana.* |
| V3 | passive | ***Built** in 1890, the bridge is still in use.* |
| having + V3 | active, earlier action | ***Having finished** the report, he left.* |
| having been + V3 | passive, earlier action | ***Having been warned**, they took precautions.* |
| not + participle | negative | ***Not knowing** what to do, I called my mother.* |

## adverbial | uses | Adverbial participle clauses: time, reason, result, condition
> ex: **After graduating**, she moved to Toronto. || time; conjunction kept for clarity
> ex: **Being** the eldest, he had to look after the others. || reason
> ex: The storm destroyed the bridge, **cutting off** the village. || result
> ex: **Used** correctly, the device is completely safe. || condition (= if it is used)
> ex: **While waiting** for the bus, I read the news. || conjunction + participle

## reduced | uses | Reduced relative clauses
A defining or non-defining relative clause can often be reduced:
| Full relative clause | Reduced |
| The woman **who is speaking** is a judge. | The woman **speaking** is a judge. |
| Cars **which are made** in Japan… | Cars **made** in Japan… |
| Anyone **who wants** a ticket… | Anyone **wanting** a ticket… |
| The first person **who arrived**… | The first person **to arrive**… (infinitive after *first, last, only*, superlatives) |
!warning Use *-ing* for active meaning even with past events: *The man **driving** the car was injured* (who was driving). Do not reduce with the perfect: ~~The people having attended~~ → *The people who had attended*.

## contrasts | contrasts | Differences between similar constructions
> ex: The people **interviewing** the candidates… || they interview (active)
> ex: The people **interviewed** for the study… || they were interviewed (passive)
> ex: **Opening** the door, she saw the parcel. || same time
> ex: **Having opened** the door, she went back to the kitchen. || one action completed first

## errors | errors | Common mistakes
!! Walking down the street, the rain started. || Walking down the street, I got caught in the rain. || The participle needs the same subject as the main clause; the rain was not walking.
!! The books writing by him are popular. || The books written by him are popular. || The books receive the action, so use the past participle.
!! Having lived in Paris, she lives there for ten years. || Having lived in Paris for ten years, she speaks fluent French. || *Having* + past participle describes an earlier action that leads to the main clause.
!! Being a sunny day, we went to the beach. || It being a sunny day, we went to the beach. / As it was a sunny day, we went to the beach. || The day was sunny, not *we*: give the participle its own subject or use a full clause.
!! Finishing the report, she had sent it to her boss. || Having finished the report, she sent it to her boss. || To show that one action was completed before another, use *having* + past participle.
## advanced | advanced | Advanced usage and stylistic nuances
- Participle clauses with their own subject (absolute constructions, formal/literary): ***The work finished**, we went home.* ***Weather permitting**, the match will go ahead.*
- Fixed participle expressions: *generally speaking, judging by, considering, given (that), assuming (that), provided*.
- *With* + noun + participle: ***With prices rising**, families are cutting back.*

## register | register | Formal and academic use
Participle clauses are common in academic, journalistic and descriptive writing: *Drawing on survey data, this study examines… / Compared with 2010, unemployment has fallen.* In conversation, full clauses are more natural.

## summary | summary | Summary
- -ing = active; V3 = passive; having + V3 = earlier action.
- They replace adverbial clauses (time, reason, condition, result) and relative clauses.
- The implied subject must match the main clause subject.
- *first/last/only* + to-infinitive in reduced relatives.

=== check
### [mc] ___ by the noise, the baby started to cry.
- Waking
+ Woken
- Having woken
> The baby received the action (it was woken), so the past participle.
### [mc] Which sentence is correct?
- Arriving at the hotel, our room was not ready.
+ Arriving at the hotel, we found that our room was not ready.
- Arrived at the hotel, our room was not ready.
> The subject of the main clause (*we*) must be the one arriving.
### [mc] The woman ___ to the manager is my sister.
+ talking
- talked
- is talking
> Reduced relative clause: *who is talking* → *talking*.
### [explain] What is the difference between *Having read the report, I called her* and *Reading the report, I called her*?
= *Having read* shows that I finished the report before calling. *Reading* suggests the two actions happened at the same time.
> The perfect participle marks an earlier action.

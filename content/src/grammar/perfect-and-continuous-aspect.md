## overview | overview | Definition and function
English verbs combine **tense** (present or past) with **aspect**. Aspect shows how the speaker views the action:
- **Simple**: the action is seen as a complete whole or a fact.
- **Continuous** (*be* + *-ing*): the action is seen as in progress, temporary or unfinished.
- **Perfect** (*have* + past participle): the action is seen from a later point and connected to it.
> ex: I **read** the report. || Simple: a complete fact.
> ex: I **was reading** the report when you called. || Continuous: in progress at that moment.
> ex: I**'ve read** the report, so I can comment. || Perfect: past action relevant now.
> ex: I**'ve been reading** reports all morning. || Perfect continuous: activity up to now, focus on duration.

## form | form | Formation
!rule Present perfect = **have/has + past participle**. Present perfect continuous = **have/has been + -ing**. The auxiliary *have* does all the grammatical work: it carries negation, questions and short answers.
#### Affirmative
| Subject | Simple | Continuous |
| I / you / we / they | **have** ('ve) **finished** | **have been** ('ve been) **working** |
| he / she / it | **has** ('s) **finished** | **has been** ('s been) **working** |
#### Negative
| Subject | Simple | Continuous |
| I / you / we / they | **haven't** (have not) **finished** | **haven't been working** |
| he / she / it | **hasn't** (has not) **finished** | **hasn't been working** |
#### Questions
| Question | Example |
| Have/Has + subject + past participle? | **Have** you **finished** the report? |
| Wh- + have/has + subject + past participle? | Where **has** she **gone**? |
| Have/Has + subject + been + -ing? | **Have** you **been waiting** long? |
| How long + have/has + subject + been + -ing? | How long **have** they **been living** here? |
#### Short answers
| Question | Yes | No |
| Have you seen it? | Yes, I **have**. | No, I **haven't**. |
| Has he been working? | Yes, he **has**. | No, he **hasn't**. |
!tip Short answers repeat only the auxiliary: ~~Yes, I have seen~~ → *Yes, I have.*
+++ The whole perfect family at a glance
| Form | Structure | Example |
| Present perfect simple | have/has + past participle | She **has written** three books. |
| Present perfect continuous | have/has been + -ing | She **has been writing** since 8 a.m. |
| Past perfect simple | had + past participle | They **had left** before we arrived. |
| Past perfect continuous | had been + -ing | I **had been waiting** for an hour. |
| Future perfect | will have + past participle | We **will have finished** by May. |
| Passive perfect | have/has been + past participle | The results **have been published**. |
+++
+++ Spelling of past participles and -ing forms
- Regular verbs add *-ed*: *work → worked*, *study → studied*, *stop → stopped*.
- Irregular participles must be learned: *go → gone*, *write → written*, *take → taken*, *be → been*.
- *-ing*: drop silent *-e* (*make → making*), double a final consonant after a short stressed vowel (*run → running*, *begin → beginning*).
+++

## uses | uses | Main rules: present perfect simple vs past simple
Use the **present perfect** when the past action is connected to now and no finished time is given:
- experience in an unfinished period: *I've visited Kazan twice.*
- a recent change with a present result: *The price has gone up.*
- states up to now with *for/since*: *We've known each other since school.*
Use the **past simple** with a finished time or when the story is clearly in the past:
> ex: I **lived** in Berlin in 2019. || finished time
> x: I have lived in Berlin in 2019. || A finished time expression needs the past simple.
> ex: **Have you seen** the new exhibition? ~ Yes, I **went** last week. || The conversation starts in the present perfect, then moves to past simple for details.

## time | time | Time expressions and signal words
!rule Expressions that mean "up to now" go with the present perfect. Expressions that point to a **finished** time go with the past simple.
| Present perfect | Past simple |
| **for** two years, **since** 2020 (up to now) | **two years ago**, **in** 2020 |
| **just**, **already**, **yet**, **recently**, **lately** | **yesterday**, **last week**, **then** |
| **ever**, **never**, **so far**, **up to now**, **to date** | **when I was a child**, **at 5 o'clock** |
| **this week / today / this year** (period not finished) | **this morning** (when it is now afternoon) |
#### Where the adverbs go
> ex: I've **just** sent it. || *just, already, ever, never* go between *have* and the participle.
> ex: Have you finished **yet**? / I haven't finished **yet**. || *yet* goes at the end in questions and negatives.
> ex: She's **already** left. / Has she left **already**? || *already* at the end of a question shows surprise.
> ex: We've lived here **for** ten years / **since** 2015. || *for* + a period; *since* + a starting point.
+++ for, since, ago — quick check
- *for* + length of time: *for a week*, *for ages*.
- *since* + point in time: *since Monday*, *since I moved*.
- *ago* = back from now, past simple only: *a week ago* (~~I've arrived a week ago~~).
+++
!warning *How long* + present perfect asks about duration up to now; *When* asks about a finished moment and needs the past simple: ~~When have you arrived?~~ → *When did you arrive?*

## continuous | uses | Simple or continuous? Results versus activity
The **simple** form focuses on the result or quantity; the **continuous** form focuses on the activity, its duration, or the fact that it may be unfinished.
> ex: I**'ve painted** the kitchen. || Finished: the result is a painted kitchen.
> ex: I**'ve been painting** the kitchen. || Activity: maybe unfinished; explains paint on my clothes.
> ex: She**'s written** 2,000 words today. || Quantity → simple.
> x: She's been writing 2,000 words today. || Don't use continuous with a completed number.
!tip **State verbs** (*know, believe, own, belong, seem, consist*) are normally not used in the continuous: *I've known her for years*, not ~~I've been knowing~~.

## variations | uses | Forms and variations
- **Repeated actions**: *He's been calling me all week* (irritation, repetition).
- **Temporary situations**: *I'm living with my parents at the moment* versus *I live in Riga* (permanent).
- **Dynamic uses of state verbs** change meaning: *I'm thinking about it* (considering) / *I think so* (opinion); *She's having lunch* (eating) / *She has a car* (owns).
- **This is the first time…** requires the present perfect: *This is the first time I've flown business class.*

## contrasts | contrasts | Differences between similar constructions
| Sentence | Meaning |
| *How long have you been learning English?* | asks about duration up to now |
| *How long did you learn English?* | the learning has finished |
| *I've been to Paris.* | I went and came back (experience) |
| *She's gone to Paris.* | she is there now or on her way |
| *I've worked here for two years.* | I still work here (simple, state-like) |
| *I've been working here for two years.* | almost the same; continuous stresses the ongoing activity |

## errors | errors | Common mistakes
> x: I am living here since 2018. || Russian uses the present tense here; English needs the present perfect: *I've lived / I've been living here since 2018.*
> x: When have you arrived? || Questions with *when* point to a finished time: *When did you arrive?*
> x: I've been knowing him for years. || State verb: *I've known him for years.*
> x: Since three years I work here. || *since* + point in time, *for* + period: *I've worked here for three years.*

## advanced | advanced | Advanced usage and stylistic nuances
At C1, aspect becomes a **choice that shapes meaning and tone**:
- The continuous can sound **less direct and more polite**: *I was wondering if you could help* (softer than *I wondered*).
- The continuous can show **annoyance**: *You're always interrupting me.*
- The present perfect is used in news to **introduce** a story, then the past simple gives details: *A major earthquake has struck the region. It hit at 3 a.m.*
- The perfect aspect in non-finite forms: *Having finished the report, she left*; *She seems to have forgotten.*
> ex: I**'ve been meaning** to call you. || continuous perfect for an intention that hasn't happened yet

## register | register | Formal and academic use
Academic writing uses the present perfect to **summarise research up to now** and the past simple for **a specific study**:
> ex: Several studies **have examined** the link between sleep and memory. Walker (2017) **found** that… || survey of literature → specific finding
!register In academic prose, the present perfect continuous is rarer; prefer *Research has increasingly focused on…* to ~~Researchers have been looking at…~~.

## summary | summary | Summary
- Simple = complete whole or fact; continuous = in progress or temporary; perfect = connected to a later point.
- Present perfect for unfinished periods and present relevance; past simple for finished times.
- Simple for results and quantities; continuous for activity and duration.
- State verbs avoid the continuous unless the meaning changes.

I ___ to Japan three times, but never in winter.

### [gap] @uses B2
I ___ (live) in Kazan since I was a child.

> *since* + point in time and the situation continues → present perfect (simple or continuous).

### [mc] @continuous B2
Your hands are covered in paint! What ___?
+ have you been doing
- have you done
- did you do
- do you do
> The paint is visible evidence of a recent activity → present perfect continuous.

- in Vienna
- I didn't see you
- at the meeting
> *When* asks about a finished time → *When did you arrive?*

### [gap] @continuous B2+
She's a fast writer: she ___ (write) four chapters this month.
= has written | 's written
> A completed number → present perfect simple, not continuous.

### [mc] @variations B2+

### [mc] @advanced C1
Which sentence sounds most tentative and polite in an email to a professor?

- I wonder whether you have time to read my draft.
- I wondered whether you had time to read my draft.
- Do you have time to read my draft?
> The past continuous *I was wondering* distances the request and softens it.

### [mc] @register C1
Choose the best opening for a literature review.
+ A number of studies have investigated the effects of screen time on sleep.
- A number of studies investigated the effects of screen time on sleep last year.
- A number of studies are investigating the effects of screen time on sleep since 2010.
- A number of studies investigate the effects of screen time on sleep until now.
> Summarising research up to the present → present perfect.

### [rewrite] @advanced C1
Rewrite using a perfect participle (begin with *Having*). ‘After she had checked the data, she submitted the paper.’ → ‘___ the data, she submitted the paper.’
= Having checked
> A perfect participle clause shows that one action was completed before the next.

### [context] @advanced C2
‘I ___ to ring you for weeks, but something always gets in the way.’ (an intention not yet carried out)
= have been meaning | 've been meaning
> *mean* in the perfect continuous expresses a long-standing intention not yet fulfilled.

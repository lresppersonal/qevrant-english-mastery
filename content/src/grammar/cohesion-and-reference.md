## core | core | Core concept
A text holds together when each sentence **picks up** something from the one before. Linkers like *however* help, but most cohesion comes from reference words that point back: *this shift, such policies, these findings, the former*. The reader should always be able to answer "what does *this* refer to?"
!rule Begin a sentence with what the reader already knows (given) and end with what is new. Use a **summary noun** after *this/these* to make the reference precise: *This **decision**…*, *These **figures**…*.
> ex: The council banned cars from the centre. **This decision** was unpopular at first. || *this* + summary noun points back clearly
> ex: Cycling and walking both increased; **the latter** rose by 20%. || *the latter* = the second of two

## overview | overview | Overview and purpose
Linking words (*however, therefore*) are only one source of cohesion. Most of the connection between sentences comes from **reference**: *This shift, such policies, the former, these findings, the latter approach*. Readers constantly ask "what does *this* refer to?". If the answer is unclear, the argument breaks down even when each sentence is grammatical.

## theory | theory | How it works: pointing back and the given-new contract
Reference words create chains across sentences:
- **Personal reference**: *it, they, them, its* — refer to a specific noun.
- **Demonstrative reference**: *this, that, these, those* — refer to nouns **or** to whole ideas.
- **Comparative reference**: *such, similar, other, the same, the former / the latter*.
- **Lexical reference**: a new noun that summarises the previous idea: *This **decline** ..., The **proposal** ..., These **findings** ...*
English prose also follows a **given-new contract**: a sentence usually starts with information the reader already has (given) and ends with new information. Reference words typically sit at the **start** of the sentence, linking back, while the new point comes at the end.
!rule In formal writing, follow *this/these* with a noun when the reference could be unclear: *This **trend** ...* rather than *This ...* alone.

## form | form | Formation: the main patterns
| Device | Pattern | Example |
| demonstrative + summary noun | this/these + noun | *Prices rose 8%. **This increase** ...* |
| such | such + (a) noun | ***Such measures** are rarely popular.* |
| the former / the latter | refer to the first / second of two | *We tested X and Y; **the latter** proved more reliable.* |
| the same / similar | comparison with something mentioned | *Other cities report **similar** problems.* |
| one(s) | substitute for a countable noun | *The old model is cheaper than **the new one**.* |
| do so / so / not | substitute for a verb phrase or clause | *Few firms **did so**. I hope **so**. I'm afraid **not**.* |
| respectively | matches two lists in order | *Groups A and B scored 61% and 74% **respectively**.* |
#### Common summary nouns
*approach, argument, assumption, claim, concern, development, difficulty, distinction, factor, finding, issue, method, pattern, phenomenon, process, shift, situation, tendency, trend, view*. Choosing a precise summary noun also **evaluates** the previous content: *this **claim*** (doubtful), *this **finding*** (established), *this **problem*** (negative).

## uses | uses | Main uses
#### 1. Avoiding repetition without losing clarity
*The government introduced a sugar tax in 2018. The tax / This levy / Such a measure ...* Each choice keeps the topic but varies the wording.
#### 2. Evaluating while referring
*Some argue that homework is unnecessary. This **view** ignores ... / This **claim** is weakly supported ...* The noun lets you judge the idea as you return to it.
#### 3. Building paragraphs
Topic sentence → development sentences beginning with given information → conclusion: *Remote work has grown rapidly. **This growth** has been uneven, however. **The largest increases** occurred in ...*
#### 4. Managing two items
*the former / the latter* for exactly two items; for more than two use *the first, the second, the last*. *Respectively* pairs two lists.

## examples | examples | Examples in context, explained
> ex: Unemployment fell while wages stagnated. **This combination** puzzled economists. || summary noun for two linked facts
> ex: Two models were tested: a linear one and a neural network. **The latter** performed better. || *the latter* = the neural network
> ex: Several councils have banned cars from school streets. **Such schemes** reduce pollution at peak times. || *such* + plural noun generalises
> ex: The survey had a low response rate. **This limitation** should be borne in mind. || summary noun evaluates the fact as a weakness
> ex: Men and women reported 6.1 and 7.4 hours of sleep, **respectively**. || order matches
> ex: Many students intend to study abroad, but few **do so**. || *do so* replaces *study abroad*
> ex: **These findings** suggest that ... || standard academic back-reference

## contrasts | contrasts | Comparison with related structures
| Pair | Difference |
| *this* vs *that* | *this* = close, current, the writer's focus; *that* = more distant or the other person's point: *That is not what I said.* In academic writing *this* is far more frequent for back-reference. |
| *this* vs *it* | *it* refers to a specific noun; *this* can refer to a whole preceding idea: *She resigned. **This** surprised everyone* (the fact of resigning). *It surprised everyone* is possible but less clearly about the whole event. |
| *such* vs *so* | *such* + noun (*such problems*); *so* + adjective (*so serious*). |
| *the latter* vs *the last* | *latter* = second of two only; for three or more, *the last*. |
| *same* vs *similar* | identical vs alike: *the same results* / *similar results*. |

## register | register | Register and stylistic nuance
!register Academic and professional writing relies on summary nouns and *such*; conversation relies on *it, that, this thing, stuff like that*. *The former/the latter* and *respectively* are formal and best used in writing.
Overusing bare *this* (*This shows that this is why this happens*) is a common weakness. One clear summary noun is worth several vague pronouns.

## errors | errors | Common mistakes
!! The company cut costs and closed two factories. This caused protests. (unclear) || The closure of the factories caused protests. || *This* alone is ambiguous here; name what you mean with a summary noun.
!! There are three options; the latter is the cheapest. || There are three options; the last is the cheapest. || *The former / the latter* refer to one of exactly two things.
!! Such a policies are unpopular. || Such policies are unpopular. / Such a policy is unpopular. || *Such a* + singular; *such* + plural or uncountable.
!! Pollution is a serious problem. These problem affects cities. || Pollution is a serious problem. This problem affects cities. || *This* + singular; *these* + plural.
!! The results were good and it showed that the method works. || The results were good, which showed that the method works. || *It* is unclear; *which* refers back to the whole previous clause.
## advanced | advanced | Advanced applications
#### Encapsulation and evaluation
Summary nouns can encapsulate a whole paragraph and steer the reader's view: *This **oversimplification** ...*, *This **paradox** ...*, *This **apparent contradiction** ...*. Choosing between *claim, argument, finding, assumption* is a stance decision.
#### Thematic progression
Advanced writers alternate patterns: (1) the same theme continues (*Remote work ... It ... It ...*); (2) the new information of one sentence becomes the theme of the next (*... caused a shortage. **This shortage** ...*). Pattern (2) creates forward momentum in argument.
#### Cataphoric reference
Reference can point forward: ***This** is the key point: costs are rising faster than revenue.* *The following* and colons create anticipation in presentations and reports.

## summary | summary | Summary
- Reference links sentences: *it, this, such, the former/latter, one, do so*.
- Use *this/these* + a summary noun when reference could be unclear.
- Summary nouns (*trend, claim, finding*) both refer and evaluate.
- Start sentences with given information; end with new information.
- *The latter* only for two items; *respectively* matches two lists in order.

### [error] @errors C1
Correct the sentence: *There are four candidates, and the latter has the most experience.*
= There are four candidates, and the last has the most experience. | There are four candidates, and the last one has the most experience.

### [mc] @uses C1
Which summary noun signals that the writer doubts the idea?
+ This claim is not supported by the data.
- This finding is supported by the data.
- This result is supported by the data.
- This fact is supported by the data.
> *Claim* presents the idea as contestable.

### [transform] @form C1
Rewrite using *respectively*: *The first group scored 61% and the second group scored 74%.*
= The first and second groups scored 61% and 74%, respectively. | The first and second groups scored 61% and 74% respectively.
> *Respectively* pairs two lists in order.

### [gap] @examples C1
Many people plan to retire early, but few actually do ___.
= so
> *Do so* replaces the verb phrase *retire early*.

### [mc] @theory C2
Which order follows the given-new principle best?
+ The council approved the plan. This decision surprised residents.
- The council approved the plan. Residents were surprised by the council approving the plan decision.
- Residents surprised this decision of the council approving the plan.
- The plan was approved. The surprise of residents was this decision.
> The second sentence starts with given information (*this decision*) and ends with the new point.

### [mc] @advanced C2
Which summary noun best encapsulates *Wealthier countries recycle more but also produce more waste*?
+ This paradox
- This finding of
- This plan
- This recommendation
> *Paradox* captures the apparent contradiction.

### [context] @register C2
Choose the clearest academic continuation: *Participants who slept less made more errors. ___*
+ This pattern held across all age groups.
- This held.
- It held across that.
- This thing held across all of them.
> A summary noun makes reference unambiguous.

### [error] @errors C2
Correct the sentence: *Such a measures have been tried before.*
= Such measures have been tried before.
> *Such a* + singular; *such* + plural.

### [rewrite] @advanced C1
Make the reference clear with a summary noun: *Prices rose while demand fell. This worried the board.*
= Prices rose while demand fell. This combination worried the board. | Prices rose while demand fell. This combination of trends worried the board.
> A summary noun specifies what *this* refers to.

### [explain] What is the "given-new" principle, and why does it make a text easier to read?
= Each sentence starts with information the reader already has and ends with new information. The reader can link each new idea to something familiar, so the argument flows.
> Reference words like *this, such* and *these* usually carry the given information at the start.

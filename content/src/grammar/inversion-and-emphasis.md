## overview | overview | Definition and function
Emphasis structures change the normal order of information to highlight one part of the message. They are typical of persuasive essays, presentations and careful conversation.

## clefts | form | Cleft sentences
| Type | Pattern | Example |
| it-cleft | It + be + focus + that/who… | ***It was the cost that** surprised me.* |
| what-cleft | What + clause + be + focus | ***What surprised me was** the cost.* |
| all-cleft | All + clause + be + focus | ***All I want is** a quiet weekend.* |
| reason/place/time | The reason/place/thing… + be | ***The reason (why) I left was** the pay.* |
> ex: **It wasn't until** midnight **that** they finally agreed. || *It was not until…* = only at that time

## do | uses | Auxiliary do for emphasis
Stressed *do/does/did* before the main verb contradicts or confirms strongly:
> ex: I **did** send the email, I promise!
> ex: She **does** work hard, even if it doesn't look like it.
> ex: **Do** sit down. || polite insistence

## inversion | form | Inversion after negative and limiting adverbials
When a negative or restrictive expression comes first, use **question word order** (auxiliary + subject):
| Adverbial | Example |
| Never / Rarely / Seldom | ***Rarely have I seen** such commitment.* |
| Not only… but also | ***Not only did** she win, **but** she **also** broke the record.* |
| No sooner… than / Hardly… when | ***No sooner had** we arrived **than** it started to rain.* |
| Only then / Only after / Only when | ***Only when** the results came **did we realise** our mistake.* |
| Under no circumstances / On no account | ***Under no circumstances should you** share your password.* |
| Little | ***Little did they know** what was coming.* |
| So / Such | ***So severe was** the damage **that** the bridge closed.* |
!warning With *Only after / Only when / Not until*, invert the **main** clause, not the time clause: *Only when I got home **did I notice** the cut.*

## fronting | uses | Fronting and tailing
- **Fronting** moves an element to the start for contrast or emphasis: ***This** I cannot accept. **Strange** as it may seem, he was right.*
- **Tailing** adds the topic at the end in speech: *It's lovely, **that beach**.* *He's very clever, **your brother**.*
- **Concessive fronting**: *Much as I admire her, I disagree. Try as he might, he couldn't open it.*

## contrasts | contrasts | Differences between similar constructions
> ex: I've never seen such chaos. || neutral
> ex: Never have I seen such chaos. || formal, dramatic
> ex: Jack broke the window. || neutral
> ex: It was Jack who broke the window. || contrastive: Jack, not someone else
> ex: What Jack did was break the window. || focus on the action

## errors | errors | Common mistakes
> x: Never I have seen such a mess. || Invert: *Never have I seen…*
> x: Not only she sings, but she also dances. || *Not only does she sing…*
> x: Only when I got home I noticed the cut. || *…did I notice the cut*.

## register | register | Formal and academic use
Inversion is formal and should be used sparingly in essays, typically once or twice for impact, especially in introductions and conclusions: *Not only does tourism create jobs, but it also funds conservation.* Clefts are useful in academic writing to signal contrast: *It is this assumption that the present study challenges.*

## summary | summary | Summary
- It-clefts and what-clefts move focus.
- Emphatic *do* confirms or contradicts.
- Negative/limiting adverbial first → auxiliary + subject.
- Fronting and tailing for contrast and spoken style.

## overview | overview | Overview and purpose
In English the verb agrees with its **subject** in number: *The report **is** late*, *The reports **are** late*. At B1 this looks trivial. At C1 it is one of the most frequent errors in academic writing, because real subjects are long (*The impact of these policies on rural communities…*), because some nouns are grammatically singular but refer to groups (*the government, the staff*), and because quantifiers and coordinated phrases follow their own logic.
!rule Find the **head noun** of the subject and make the verb agree with it, not with the noun closest to the verb. Then check whether the meaning (notional agreement) overrides the grammar.

## theory | theory | How it works: grammatical and notional agreement
English uses two principles, and good writers know when each applies.
- **Grammatical agreement**: the verb follows the form of the head noun. *The list of candidates **is** long* (head = *list*).
- **Notional agreement**: the verb follows the meaning. *A number of candidates **have** withdrawn* (the meaning is "several candidates", so plural), *Ten kilometres **is** a long way* (one distance, so singular).
- **Proximity agreement**: in a few structures the verb agrees with the nearest noun: *Either the director or the trustees **are** responsible.*
In formal writing, grammatical agreement is the default. Notional agreement is accepted where the meaning is clearly singular or plural, and proximity agreement is standard only after *either…or*, *neither…nor*, *not only…but also* and in *there is/are* sentences in informal speech.

## form | form | Formation: patterns to check
| Subject type | Verb | Example |
| head noun + of-phrase | agrees with the head | *The quality of the results **depends** on the sample.* |
| the number of + plural | singular | *The number of applicants **has** doubled.* |
| a number of + plural | plural | *A number of applicants **have** complained.* |
| each / every + noun | singular | *Each student **receives** a copy.* |
| each of / none of / neither of + plural | singular (formal); plural (informal) | *None of the proposals **was** accepted.* / *None of them **were** ready.* |
| most / half / all / some of + noun | agrees with the noun after *of* | *Most of the money **was** spent. Most of the funds **were** spent.* |
| A and B | plural | *Funding and staffing **are** the main problems.* |
| A and B as one idea | singular | *Fish and chips **is** a British classic.* |
| either A or B / neither A nor B | nearest noun | *Neither the manager nor the employees **were** told.* |
| A as well as / along with / together with B | agrees with A | *The minister, as well as her advisers, **was** criticised.* |
| clause or gerund as subject | singular | *What we need **is** more time. Reducing waste **saves** money.* |
| amounts of time, money, distance | singular | *Three years **is** a long time to wait.* |

## uses | uses | Main uses and decisions
#### 1. Long subjects: find the head
In *The rise in the cost of materials and labour **has** delayed the project*, the head is *rise*, so the verb is singular even though *materials and labour* is closest. Reading the subject aloud without its modifiers (*The rise… has delayed*) is a reliable check.
#### 2. Collective nouns: one unit or several individuals?
Nouns such as *government, team, committee, staff, family, audience, public, police* can take a singular or plural verb in British English, depending on whether the group acts as **one body** or as **individuals**: *The committee **has** approved the budget* (a single decision); *The committee **are** divided on the issue* (members with different views). American English strongly prefers the singular. *Police, people, cattle* are always plural: *The police **are** investigating.*
!register In formal academic writing, choose one form and keep pronouns consistent: *The government **has** changed **its** policy* or *The government **have** changed **their** policy* — not *has… their*.
#### 3. Quantifiers: look past "of"
With *most, all, some, half, a lot, the majority, a third, plenty* + *of*, the verb agrees with the noun that follows: *A third of the land **is** protected; a third of the species **are** endangered.* With *the majority of* + plural, plural is usual (*The majority of voters **were** undecided*), but *The majority **is** clear* (the majority as a single group) is also possible.
#### 4. Coordination and correlatives
Two subjects joined by *and* are plural, unless they form a single idea (*Research and development **is** a priority* can be read as one function). After *or/nor*, the nearest subject wins; if that produces an awkward sentence, place the plural noun second: *Neither the chair nor the members **were** consulted.*
#### 5. Plural-looking and Latin/Greek nouns
*News, physics, economics (the subject), measles, the United States* are singular. *Data* is plural in traditional academic style (*The data **suggest**…*) but widely singular as a mass noun (*The data **is** stored online*). *Criteria, phenomena, hypotheses* are plural; *criterion, phenomenon, hypothesis* are singular. *Statistics* is singular as a subject of study and plural for figures.

## examples | examples | Examples in context, explained
> ex: The **impact** of social media on adolescents' sleep **has** been widely studied. || head = *impact*; *adolescents* is inside a modifier
> ex: **The number** of international students **has** fallen for the third year running. || *the number* is the head: singular
> ex: **A number of** universities **have** responded by cutting fees. || *a number of* = several: plural
> ex: **None of** the proposed solutions **addresses** the underlying problem. || formal singular after *none of*
> ex: The **staff are** unhappy about the new rota. || BrE plural: the staff as individuals with feelings
> ex: **What** the region needs **is** investment, not slogans. || a *what*-clause subject is singular, even before a plural complement
> ex: **Neither** the survey **nor** the interviews **were** designed to measure this. || proximity: nearest noun = *interviews*
> ex: The **data suggest** that the effect is small. || traditional academic plural *data*

## contrasts | contrasts | Comparison with related structures
| Pair | Difference |
| *the number of* vs *a number of* | *the number* = the figure (singular); *a number of* = several (plural). |
| *each student* vs *the students each* | *Each student **has**…* (singular) vs *The students each **have**…* (plural subject, *each* is an adverb). |
| *none of them is* vs *none of them are* | both used; *is* is more formal and expected in academic writing, *are* is normal in speech. |
| *The family is* vs *The family are* | the family as a unit vs its members as individuals (BrE). |
| *There is a pen and two books* vs *There are a pen and two books* | informal English agrees with the first noun (*is*); careful writing often rewrites: *There are two books and a pen.* |
| *more than one* vs *more than two* | *More than one student **has** complained* (singular) but *More than two students **have** complained*. |

## register | register | Register and stylistic nuance
!register Academic style favours **grammatical** agreement and singular verbs after *none, neither, each* and *either*. Conversation freely uses plural (*Neither of them are coming*), and this is not a mistake in speech.
Informal speech also uses *there's* with plural nouns (*There's three reasons*), which careful writing avoids. In BrE journalism plural verbs with organisations are common (*Arsenal **are** through to the final*, *the BBC **have** apologised*); in academic and American English the singular is safer.

## errors | errors | Common mistakes advanced learners make
> x: The results of the survey shows a clear pattern. || The results of the survey **show** a clear pattern. (head = *results*)
> x: The number of cars have increased. || The number of cars **has** increased.
> x: Every student and teacher have a card. || Every student and teacher **has** a card. (*every* makes the subject singular)
> x: One of the main reasons are cost. || One of the main reasons **is** cost.
> x: The police has arrested a suspect. || The police **have** arrested a suspect.
> x: These criteria is not met. || These criteria **are** not met. (*criterion* is the singular)
!warning Watch *one of the + plural noun + who/that*: *She is **one of the researchers who have** questioned this* (the relative clause describes *researchers*). *She is **the only one** of the researchers **who has**…* is singular because the antecedent is *the only one*.

## advanced | advanced | Advanced applications
#### Agreement in pseudo-clefts and inverted sentences
In *What worries economists **is** the falling birth rate*, the verb agrees with the *what*-clause (singular). With a plural complement, both *is* and *are* occur: *What we need **is/are** more teachers*; formal writing prefers *is*. In inversion, agreement is with the postponed subject: *Of greater concern **are** the long-term effects. Among the survivors **was** a young doctor.*
#### Agreement across parentheses
Parenthetical phrases do not change the subject: *The government, **like** most of its European partners, **has** resisted the proposal.* If both items should count, use *and*: *The government and most of its partners **have** resisted…*
#### Choosing to rewrite
When both options sound awkward (*Neither he nor I am/are…*), advanced writers rewrite: *He is not responsible, and neither am I.*

## summary | summary | Summary
- Agree with the **head noun**, not the nearest noun.
- *The number of* + singular verb; *a number of* + plural verb.
- Quantifier + *of*: agree with the noun after *of* (*most of the money is / most of the funds are*).
- Collective nouns: singular for a unit, plural (BrE) for individuals; be consistent with pronouns.
- *Either/neither…or/nor*: agree with the nearest subject.
- Clauses, gerunds and amounts are singular.

## overview | overview | Overview and purpose
Adjectives and adverbs carry much of the precision in advanced English: *a **marginally** better result*, *a **deeply** flawed argument*, *an **utterly** convincing case*. Three systems decide whether these combinations sound natural: **gradability** (which intensifiers an adjective accepts), **position** (where an adverb sits and what it modifies) and **order** (how several adjectives line up before a noun).

## theory | theory | How it works: gradable and non-gradable adjectives
**Gradable** adjectives describe qualities that exist in degrees: *cold, interesting, important*. You can have more or less of them, so they combine with **grading adverbs**: *very, extremely, fairly, rather, slightly, a bit*.
**Non-gradable** adjectives describe either an **extreme** (*freezing, fascinating, essential*) or an **absolute/classifying** category (*unique, impossible, dead, nuclear, annual*). They already contain the idea of "maximum" or "either-or", so *very* sounds illogical: *~~very freezing~~, ~~very unique~~*. Instead they take **non-grading adverbs**: *absolutely, completely, totally, utterly* (extremes) or *almost, nearly, virtually, practically* (absolutes).
!rule *Really* and *pretty* work with both types in informal English; *quite* changes meaning: *quite good* = fairly good (gradable), *quite brilliant* = completely brilliant (non-gradable).

## form | form | Formation: intensifier patterns
| Adjective type | Typical adverbs | Examples |
| gradable | very, extremely, highly, fairly, rather, slightly, somewhat, a little | *highly unlikely, fairly typical, slightly disappointing* |
| extreme | absolutely, utterly, totally, really, quite | *absolutely essential, utterly exhausted, quite extraordinary* |
| absolute/classifying | completely, entirely, almost, virtually, practically | *entirely new, virtually impossible, almost unique* |
#### Strong adverb–adjective collocations
Advanced English relies on fixed pairings rather than *very*: *bitterly disappointed, deeply concerned, highly controversial, painfully slow, strikingly similar, fundamentally different, widely available, readily accessible, acutely aware, sorely missed, vastly superior, heavily dependent.*
#### Adverb positions
- **front**: connecting and comment adverbs: *Fortunately, the data were saved.*
- **mid** (before the main verb, after be/first auxiliary): frequency, focus, certainty: *She has **probably** left. They **rarely** reply.*
- **end**: manner, place, time: *He spoke **quietly** in the hall **yesterday**.*
#### Adjective order before a noun
opinion → size → age → shape → colour → origin → material → purpose + noun: *a **beautiful old Italian marble** fountain*, *a **small round wooden** table*. Usually no more than three adjectives appear together in natural English.

## uses | uses | Main uses
#### 1. Precision instead of emphasis
*very important* is vague; *critically important*, *of central importance* or *paramount* are precise. In formal writing, prefer a stronger adjective or a collocating adverb to stacking *very*.
#### 2. Downtoning and hedging
*somewhat, slightly, relatively, fairly, comparatively* soften claims: *The effect was **relatively** small.* Academic writing uses these to avoid overstatement.
#### 3. Focus through position
Moving an adverb changes what it modifies: *She **only** teaches on Fridays* (and nothing else on Fridays? or only on Fridays?) is ambiguous in writing; *She teaches **only** on Fridays* is precise.
#### 4. Comment versus manner
*She answered the question **honestly**.* (manner: how she answered) / ***Honestly**, she answered the question.* (comment: the speaker's frankness). Front position turns many manner adverbs into comment adverbs: *clearly, frankly, naturally, seriously.*

## examples | examples | Examples in context, explained
> ex: The proposal is **highly controversial** and **deeply unpopular** with staff. || adverb–adjective collocations instead of *very*
> ex: It is **virtually impossible** to verify the claim. || absolute adjective + *virtually*
> ex: The two cases are **strikingly similar** in their outcomes. || *strikingly* adds the idea of noticeability
> ex: I was **absolutely furious**, not just annoyed. || extreme adjective + *absolutely*
> ex: The results are **broadly consistent** with earlier studies. || academic hedging: *broadly* = in general
> ex: **Clearly**, the policy has failed. / He didn't explain it **clearly**. || comment vs manner
> ex: a **charming little eighteenth-century stone** cottage || opinion → size → age → material

## contrasts | contrasts | Comparison with related structures
| Pair | Difference |
| *quite good* / *quite perfect* | moderately vs completely (depends on gradability) |
| *hard* / *hardly* | *hard* = with effort (*work hard*); *hardly* = almost not (*I hardly know her*) |
| *late* / *lately* | *arrive late* vs *recently* (*I haven't slept well lately*) |
| *high* / *highly* | literal height vs degree: *aim high* / *highly recommended* |
| *free* / *freely* | without payment vs without restriction: *travel free* / *speak freely* |
| *most* / *mostly* | superlative vs mainly: *the most useful* / *mostly useful* |
| *fairly* / *rather* | *fairly* is neutral or positive; *rather* often suggests surprise or criticism: *rather expensive* |

## register | register | Register and stylistic nuance
!register Informal: *really, so, super, pretty, totally*. Neutral: *very, quite, extremely*. Formal/academic: *highly, considerably, substantially, markedly, relatively, comparatively, somewhat*.
In academic writing, intensifiers are used sparingly; overuse of *extremely* or *absolutely* sounds emotional. Precision comes from evidence and from exact collocations: *a **statistically significant** difference*, *a **markedly** higher rate*.

## errors | errors | Common mistakes advanced learners make
> x: The view was very breathtaking. || The view was absolutely breathtaking.
> x: This is a very unique opportunity. || This is a unique opportunity. / This is a truly unique opportunity.
> x: She speaks very well English. || She speaks English very well.
> x: I hardly worked to finish the project. || I worked hard to finish the project.
> x: a wooden old big table || a big old wooden table
> x: The results were highly disappointed. || The results were highly disappointing.

## advanced | advanced | Advanced applications
#### Intensifying with structure rather than adverbs
*Not only is the method slow, it is **far from** reliable.* *Nothing could be **further from** the truth.* *The difference is **by no means** trivial.* Litotes and negated adverbs give controlled emphasis in formal prose.
#### Adjective complementation
Many adjectives require specific patterns: *aware **of***, *conducive **to***, *consistent **with***, *reluctant **to do***, *it is imperative **that** ...*. Getting the complement right matters as much as the intensifier.
#### Post-positive adjectives
Some adjectives follow the noun: *the people **involved**, the members **present**, the issues **concerned**, something **new**, the best solution **available**.* The position can change meaning: *the **concerned** parent* (worried) vs *the parent **concerned*** (the one in question).

## summary | summary | Summary
- Gradable adjectives take *very, extremely, fairly*; extreme and absolute adjectives take *absolutely, completely, virtually*.
- Learn adverb–adjective collocations (*deeply concerned, highly unlikely*) instead of *very*.
- Adverb position affects focus and meaning; front position often gives a comment adverb.
- Adjective order: opinion, size, age, shape, colour, origin, material, purpose.
- Formal writing prefers precise adverbs (*markedly, considerably, relatively*) and avoids emotional intensifiers.

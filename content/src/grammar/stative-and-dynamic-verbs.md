## overview | overview | Overview and purpose
Verbs describe either **actions and events** (dynamic: *run, write, explain, become*) or **states** (stative: *know, belong, contain, seem*). The difference matters because continuous forms present a situation as **in progress, temporary or developing**, and a state is usually none of those things. *I know the answer* describes a stable condition; *~~I'm knowing the answer~~* makes no sense because knowing does not unfold over time.
!rule A verb is not "stative" in the dictionary. A **use** of a verb is stative or dynamic. *Have* is stative in *I have two sisters* but dynamic in *I'm having lunch*.

## theory | theory | How it works: why states resist the continuous
The continuous (be + -ing) adds three possible meanings: **duration in progress** (*she's writing*), **temporariness** (*I'm living with friends this month*) and **change or development** (*it's getting colder*). States are, by default, unbounded and unchanging, so none of these meanings applies. That is why learners are taught to avoid the continuous with verbs of:
- **thinking and knowing**: *know, believe, understand, realise, suppose, doubt, recognise*
- **feeling and wanting**: *like, love, hate, prefer, want, wish, need*
- **possession and relation**: *own, belong, possess, include, contain, consist of, depend on, involve, lack*
- **perception (involuntary)**: *see, hear, smell, taste* (as states)
- **being and appearance**: *be, seem, appear, resemble, mean, matter, deserve*
When a speaker *does* use the continuous with one of these verbs, it is a deliberate choice: the state is being reframed as an **activity**, as **temporary**, as **changing**, or as **intensely felt right now**. The grammar is not broken; the meaning has shifted.

## form | form | Formation: verbs with two uses
| Verb | Stative use (simple) | Dynamic use (continuous possible) |
| think | opinion: *I **think** it's a mistake.* | mental activity: *I'**m thinking** about changing jobs.* |
| have | possession, relation: *She **has** a PhD.* | experience, action: *We'**re having** a meeting / a hard time.* |
| see | perceive: *I **see** the problem.* | meet, date: *She'**s seeing** a consultant.* |
| taste, smell | quality: *The soup **tastes** salty.* | deliberate action: *He'**s tasting** the soup.* |
| be | permanent quality: *He **is** rude.* | temporary behaviour: *He'**s being** rude* (today, on purpose). |
| appear | seem: *She **appears** calm.* | perform, take part: *She'**s appearing** in a new play.* |
| weigh, measure | quantity: *The case **weighs** 20 kg.* | action: *They'**re weighing** the luggage.* |
| expect | believe: *I **expect** you're tired.* | wait for: *We'**re expecting** a baby / a delivery.* |
| depend | relation: *It **depends** on the weather.* | (informal) progressive of uncertainty: *It's **depending** more and more on exports.* (change) |

## uses | uses | Main uses: when the continuous is possible with states
#### 1. Temporary or untypical behaviour
*Be* + adjective of behaviour in the continuous describes conduct at a particular time, often deliberate: *You'**re being** very unreasonable.* Compare *You **are** unreasonable* (your character).
#### 2. Change and development
States that are changing can take the continuous, especially with comparatives: *I'**m understanding** more of each lecture as the term goes on.* *Prices **are seeming** less important to buyers than reliability.* The continuous here describes a process.
#### 3. Emphasis on the present moment or strong feeling
In informal speech: *I'**m loving** this course* (enjoying it right now, intensely); *I'**m wanting** to leave soon* (a tentative, softened wish). This is natural in conversation but marked in formal writing.
#### 4. Politeness and tentativeness
The past continuous or present continuous softens a request: *I **was wondering** if you could help.* *We'**re hoping** you'll join us.* Distance in time or aspect creates social distance.
#### 5. Perfect continuous with states of duration
A few states that describe a lasting relationship accept the perfect continuous when duration is stressed: *I'**ve been wanting** to tell you this for weeks.* *She'**s been having** doubts about the project.* Most others prefer the perfect simple: *I'**ve known** him for years* (~~have been knowing~~).

## examples | examples | Examples in context, explained
> ex: The committee **consists of** twelve members. || a fixed composition, so simple
> ex: The data **suggest** that attendance falls in winter. || academic stative use: suggest = indicate
> ex: Researchers **are increasingly recognising** the role of sleep in memory. || a developing trend, so continuous with an adverb of change
> ex: I **was thinking** we could split the bill. || polite, tentative suggestion
> ex: The new manager **is proving** surprisingly popular. || prove = turn out to be, developing over time
> ex: He**'s being** deliberately vague, which isn't like him. || temporary behaviour, contrasted with his character
> ex: I **see** what you mean, but I'**m** still **seeing** the dentist on Friday. || perceive vs meet (arrangement)

## contrasts | contrasts | Comparison with related structures
| Pair | Difference |
| *I think* vs *I'm thinking* | opinion vs active consideration. *I'm thinking we should leave* is an informal, tentative opinion. |
| *She is difficult* vs *She is being difficult* | character vs behaviour now. The second often implies the behaviour is unusual or deliberate. |
| *I've known* vs *I've been knowing* | the second is not used. Duration with most states uses the perfect simple. |
| *It depends* vs *It's depending* | the second only describes a developing dependency (rare, informal). |
| *I can see* vs *I'm seeing* | for perception in progress, English uses **can** + see/hear: *I can hear music* (not *~~I'm hearing music~~*). *I'm hearing* means *I'm being told* (*I'm hearing good things about her*). |

## register | register | Register and stylistic nuance
!register In academic writing, use stative verbs in the simple form for claims: *The findings **indicate**, the model **assumes**, the sample **comprises**.* The continuous of a stative verb sounds conversational.
Informal speech uses stative continuous for **vividness**: *I'm loving it, I'm needing a break, I'm not liking this.* These are acceptable in casual conversation and marketing but avoid them in essays. In narrative, *was being* + adjective can characterise a moment: *She was being kind, not honest.*

## errors | errors | Common mistakes advanced learners make
> x: I am knowing him since 2019. || I have known him since 2019.
> x: This box is containing old letters. || This box contains old letters.
> x: I am agree with the author. || I agree with the author. (agree is a verb, not an adjective)
> x: I'm hearing a strange noise. || I can hear a strange noise.
> x: The results are depending on the sample size. || The results depend on the sample size.
!warning Over-correcting is also a mistake: *We're having a meeting* and *I'm thinking about it* are correct, because *have* and *think* are dynamic here.

## advanced | advanced | Advanced applications
#### Stative passives and resultative states
*The door **is locked*** can describe a state (it is locked now) rather than an action. Compare *The door **is locked** every night at ten* (a repeated action). Advanced writers exploit this: *The region **is** largely **populated** by farmers* is stative; *is being populated* would describe a process of settlement.
#### Dynamic uses for stance
Verbs of cognition in the continuous can signal that a view is **provisional**: *Many economists **are questioning** whether growth targets are realistic.* The continuous presents the questioning as current and evolving, which is useful for describing debates.
#### Aspect and politeness scale
*I want → I'd like → I was wondering if → I was hoping you might*: each step adds distance (modal, past, continuous). Choose the degree that matches the social situation.

## summary | summary | Summary
- Stative uses describe unchanging conditions and normally take simple forms; dynamic uses describe actions and accept the continuous.
- Many verbs (*think, have, see, be, taste, appear, expect*) have both uses with different meanings.
- With a stative verb, the continuous signals temporariness, change, emphasis or politeness.
- For duration, most states take the perfect simple: *I've known*, *it has belonged*.
- Perception in progress: *can see, can hear*.

- This software is belonging to the university.
- I'm knowing the reason for the delay.
- The report is containing three appendices.
> *Have* here means *experience*, a dynamic use. *Belong, know* and *contain* are stative.

> *Taste* + adjective describes a quality: stative, so simple.

### [gap] @uses B2
You ___ (be) very quiet tonight. Is anything wrong?
= 're being | are being
> Temporary behaviour that differs from usual: *be being* + adjective.

### [mc] @contrasts B2
I ___ about applying for the Master's programme, but I haven't decided yet.
+ 'm thinking
- think
- have thought
- am thought

### [error] @errors B2+
Correct the sentence: *I have been knowing my supervisor since my first year.*
= I have known my supervisor since my first year. | I've known my supervisor since my first year.
> Duration with a state uses the perfect simple.

### [mc] @contrasts B2+
Which sentence describes perception happening now in natural English?
+ I can hear someone in the corridor.
- I'm hearing someone in the corridor.
- I hear someone in the corridor now.
- I have heard someone in the corridor now.
> English uses *can* + hear/see for perception in progress.

### [transform] @uses B2+
Make the request more tentative, using *wonder* in the past continuous: *Can you look at my draft?*
= I was wondering if you could look at my draft. | I was wondering whether you could look at my draft.
> Past + continuous adds distance and politeness.

### [mc] @advanced C1
Which sentence presents a view as current and still developing?
+ More and more researchers are questioning the reliability of these surveys.
- More and more researchers question the reliability of these surveys.
- More and more researchers have questioned the reliability of these surveys.
- More and more researchers questioned the reliability of these surveys.
> The continuous with *more and more* frames the questioning as an evolving trend.

### [context] @register C1
In an academic essay, which verb form is most appropriate? *The sample ___ 240 undergraduates from three universities.*
+ comprises
- is comprising
- is being comprised of
- has been comprising
> *Comprise* is stative and academic writing prefers simple forms for descriptions.

### [gap] @examples C1
I ___ (want) to ask you about this for ages, but the moment never seemed right.
= 've been wanting | have been wanting
> *Want* accepts the perfect continuous when a long-held wish is emphasised.

### [mc] @advanced C2
Which sentence describes a process rather than a state?
+ The coastline is gradually being developed for tourism.
- The coastline is developed for tourism.
- The coastline is largely undeveloped.
- The coastline has been developed since the 1990s.
> *Is being developed* presents an ongoing process; *is developed* can be a resulting state.

Rewrite to show that the behaviour is unusual for him: *He is selfish.* (use *be* in the continuous)

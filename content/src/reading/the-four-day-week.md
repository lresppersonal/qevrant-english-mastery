[A] For most of the last century, the five-day, forty-hour week has been treated as a natural law of working life. Yet it is a relatively recent invention. In many industrial countries, six-day weeks and far longer hours were normal until campaigns by trade unions and changes in technology gradually reduced them. Supporters of a four-day week argue that the next step is overdue.

[B] In recent years, companies in several countries have taken part in organised trials in which employees worked four days for the same pay, on condition that productivity remained stable. Many participating firms reported that output held steady or even improved, while staff described lower stress, better sleep and more time for family. A large proportion of the companies chose to continue the arrangement after the trial ended.

[C] Why might working less not mean producing less? One explanation is that much of the working week is not spent productively at all. Long meetings, unnecessary emails and constant interruptions fill hours without adding much value. When time is limited, teams tend to cut these first. Another explanation is that rested workers make fewer mistakes and are less likely to take sick leave, which reduces hidden costs for employers.

[D] Sceptics point out that the trials have important limitations. Companies volunteered to take part, so they were probably those most likely to succeed. Many were small firms in office-based sectors such as marketing or software, where output is harder to measure and work can be reorganised relatively easily. It is much less obvious how a hospital, a school or a factory running continuous shifts could cut a day without hiring more staff.

[E] There are also questions about fairness. If office workers gain an extra day off while nurses, drivers and shop assistants do not, existing divisions in the labour market could widen. Some economists suggest that, rather than a fixed four-day week for everyone, the real lesson of the trials is that working time should be more flexible and focused on results rather than hours.

[F] What seems clear is that the debate has moved from whether shorter hours are possible to how and where they make sense. As automation takes over more routine tasks, the question of how the gains are shared, through higher wages, lower prices or more free time, is likely to become one of the defining economic arguments of the coming decades.

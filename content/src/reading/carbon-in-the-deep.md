[A] When people picture the natural systems that keep carbon out of the atmosphere, they tend to imagine forests. Far less attention is paid to the sea floor, where the remains of plankton, plants and animals settle slowly into layers of sediment. Over thousands of years, some of that organic material is buried deeply enough to be locked away, making ocean sediments one of the largest long-term carbon stores on the planet.

[B] Coastal habitats are a particularly striking case. Salt marshes, mangrove forests and seagrass meadows occupy a tiny fraction of the ocean's area, yet they are thought to bury carbon at rates per hectare that can exceed those of many land forests. Their waterlogged soils contain little oxygen, which slows the decomposition that would otherwise return carbon to the air. Scientists sometimes group these ecosystems under the label "blue carbon".

[C] The difficulty is that what has been stored can also be released. When mangroves are cleared for aquaculture, or coastal wetlands are drained for development, the exposed soils begin to break down, and carbon accumulated over centuries may be lost within years. Further offshore, there is growing debate about bottom trawling, a fishing method that drags heavy nets across the sea bed. Some studies have argued that the disturbance releases significant amounts of carbon from sediments; others question the size of the effect and how much of it reaches the atmosphere rather than remaining dissolved in seawater.

[D] This uncertainty is not a reason for complacency, but it does complicate policy. Carbon markets increasingly offer credits for protecting or restoring coastal ecosystems, which can channel money towards conservation. Critics warn, however, that measuring carbon in mud is technically demanding and that poorly verified credits could allow polluters to claim reductions that never occur.

[E] There is also a risk of treating the sea floor purely as a carbon accounting problem. Seagrass meadows shelter young fish, mangroves protect shorelines from storms, and sediments support communities of organisms that are still barely catalogued. Protecting them for carbon alone may lead to the wrong priorities, such as favouring fast-growing plantations over diverse but slower habitats.

[F] What emerges is a picture of a quiet, slow-moving system whose value has long been overlooked. The research is far from settled, yet a cautious conclusion seems reasonable: it is usually cheaper, and more certain, to keep existing stores intact than to try to rebuild them once they have been disturbed.

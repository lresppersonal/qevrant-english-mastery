[A] For most of human history, living alone was unusual. People lived with parents, spouses, children, servants or lodgers, and households of one were largely confined to widows and a few eccentrics. Historical records suggest that this began to change in early-industrialised countries more than a century ago, and the shift accelerated in the second half of the twentieth century. Today, according to data compiled by Our World in Data, one-person households account for a large share of homes in many rich countries, and in some northern European cities they make up around half of all households.

[B] The pattern is closely linked to wealth. Across countries, the share of people living alone tends to rise with national income. This is not surprising: living alone is expensive. It requires an entire home's worth of rent, heating and furniture for a single person, as well as a labour market that offers stable incomes and a welfare state or pension system that reduces dependence on family. Where those conditions are absent, sharing a household is often an economic necessity rather than a preference.

[C] Demographic change has also played a role. People marry later, divorce more often and live longer than in the past. A widowed grandmother who would once have moved in with her children may now remain in her own flat for decades. Young professionals increasingly spend a period of their twenties or thirties living independently before or instead of forming a couple. Living alone, in other words, is less often a single permanent condition than a stage that many people pass through, sometimes more than once.

[D] It is tempting to read these figures as evidence of an epidemic of isolation. Commentators frequently cite the rise of solo living alongside warnings about loneliness, implying that one causes the other. Yet the relationship is far from straightforward. Loneliness is a subjective feeling: the gap between the relationships people want and the ones they have. Living alone is simply an arrangement. Many people who live by themselves have rich social lives and value the privacy and autonomy their homes provide, while others feel profoundly lonely in crowded family homes. Analyses of survey data have not found clear evidence that loneliness has risen in step with solitary living.

[E] This does not mean the trend is free of risks. Older people living alone may be vulnerable if their health declines, and those without close relatives can struggle to find support in a crisis. In poorer countries where welfare systems are weaker, a growing number of single-person households could place new pressures on families and states. Housing markets, too, have been slow to adapt: much of the existing housing stock was designed for families, and small, affordable homes are scarce in many cities.

[F] Perhaps the most interesting question is not whether living alone is good or bad, but what kinds of community it requires. If more people live by themselves, the importance of what sociologists call "third places" — cafés, libraries, sports clubs, community centres — increases. Cities that make it easy to be alone without being isolated may turn out to be the ones best prepared for the households of the future.

[A] Few rhetorical devices are as effective as a well-placed figure. "Crime has risen by 40 per cent" lands with a force that "crime seems to be getting worse" can only envy. The number implies measurement, method and an expert somewhere who has done the counting. Precisely because it appears to have been stripped of opinion, it is rarely interrogated with the scepticism we would bring to an adjective.

[B] Yet every statistic is the residue of a series of human choices. Someone decided what to count, how to define it, over what period and against which baseline. A 40 per cent rise from twenty incidents to twenty-eight tells a different story from the same percentage applied to thousands, and a rise measured from an unusually quiet year may reflect nothing more than a return to normal. None of these choices is necessarily dishonest; most are unavoidable. But they are seldom visible in the headline, and the reader who sees only the result inherits the conclusions without the caveats.

[C] Relative figures are a particular hazard. To say that a habit "doubles the risk" of a disease sounds alarming, and may be entirely accurate, but if the underlying risk is one in a hundred thousand, the doubled risk remains vanishingly small. Researchers in risk communication have long argued that absolute numbers — how many people out of a given group are affected — are more readily understood, yet relative framings persist, not least because they make for more arresting copy.

[D] Then there is the question of what goes uncounted. Official statistics tend to capture what is easy to record: transactions, registrations, reported offences. Activity that happens informally, in unpaid care or in communities reluctant to deal with authorities, may be under-represented or absent altogether. A policy that improves the measured indicator can therefore leave the underlying reality unchanged, or even worse; when a number becomes a target, institutions learn to manage the number.

[E] None of this licenses the opposite error, a blanket cynicism in which all figures are dismissed as spin. Carefully collected data remain among the best tools societies have for checking intuitions that are frequently wrong. The point is not to trust numbers less, but to trust them more selectively: to ask what was counted, compared with what, and by whom, before allowing a single figure to settle a question.

[F] In that sense, statistical literacy is less a technical skill than a habit of mind. It consists largely of a few modest questions, asked consistently. They will not make anyone immune to persuasion, but they may at least ensure that, when we are persuaded by a number, it is because the number deserves it.

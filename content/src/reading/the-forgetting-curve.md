[A] In the 1880s, the psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus set himself an unusual task. He memorised long lists of meaningless syllables, such as "DAX" or "ZUF", and then tested how many he could still recall after different intervals. Using nonsense was deliberate: real words carry associations that make some easier to remember than others, and he wanted to measure memory in its purest form.

[B] What he found has become known as the forgetting curve. Memory loss was steepest at the beginning. Within an hour, a large part of a newly learned list had gone; after a day, more had disappeared; but after that, forgetting slowed down considerably. What remained after a week tended to stay. The exact figures varied with the material, yet the shape of the curve, a sharp drop followed by a long, flat tail, has been confirmed by researchers many times since.

[C] Ebbinghaus also noticed something more encouraging. When he relearned a list he had partly forgotten, it took him less time than the first attempt. The memory had not vanished completely; it had merely become harder to reach. Each time he reviewed the material, the curve that followed was flatter, meaning he forgot more slowly. This is the principle behind what is now called spaced repetition: rather than studying something many times in one session, you return to it at increasing intervals, ideally just before you would otherwise forget it.

[D] Later research added a second insight. Simply rereading notes feels productive, but it is a relatively weak way to learn. Testing yourself, by trying to recall an answer before checking it, strengthens memory far more. Psychologists call this retrieval practice. The effort of pulling information out of memory seems to be exactly what makes it stick. Ironically, the strategies that feel easiest, such as highlighting or rereading, often create an illusion of mastery, while the ones that feel difficult produce more durable learning.

[E] None of this means that cramming never works. A student who studies intensively the night before an exam may well pass it. The problem is what happens afterwards. Material crammed into a single session tends to follow the steep part of the forgetting curve, so much of it is lost within days. For anything that needs to last, such as a language, a professional skill or the foundations of a degree, spacing and self-testing are considerably more efficient.

[F] Digital tools have made these ideas easy to apply. Flashcard apps can calculate when each item is due for review, showing difficult words more often and easy ones less. But the underlying advice requires no technology at all: study a little, leave a gap, test yourself, and come back. Ebbinghaus's lists of nonsense, it turns out, contained a lesson that makes a great deal of sense.

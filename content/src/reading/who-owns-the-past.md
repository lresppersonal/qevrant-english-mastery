[A] Walk through the galleries of almost any major European museum and you will encounter objects from distant places: sculptures, manuscripts, ceremonial masks, human remains. Some were bought or exchanged fairly. Many others were acquired during periods of colonial rule, military conquest or unequal trade, when the people who made or owned them had little power to refuse.

[B] For decades, museums defended their collections with two main arguments. The first was legal: objects had often been acquired under the laws of the time, and national legislation sometimes forbids museums from giving items away. The second was what might be called the universal museum argument: by displaying artefacts from many cultures side by side, large museums allow visitors to see human history as a shared story, and they have the resources to conserve fragile objects.

[C] Critics find neither argument convincing. Laws written by colonial powers, they point out, can hardly settle questions of justice. And the idea of a universal museum sits uneasily with the fact that most of the world's population will never visit the cities where these collections are kept. For communities whose sacred objects or ancestors' remains are on display abroad, the issue is not access to world culture, but respect and the right to tell their own history.

[D] In recent years, the debate has shifted. A number of governments and institutions have agreed to return specific items, including bronze sculptures, carved figures and human remains, to the countries or communities they came from. Some museums have begun detailed research into the provenance of their collections, tracing how each object arrived and whether it was obtained with consent.

[E] Return, however, is rarely simple. There may be disagreement about who the rightful owner is: a national government, a royal family, a local community or the descendants of the original makers. Some objects are fragile and require specialist conditions. In other cases, the countries of origin have chosen long-term loans or joint exhibitions rather than permanent return, at least for now.

[F] What the debate reveals is that museums are not neutral spaces. The decision about what to collect, how to label it and where to keep it reflects choices about whose history matters. Whatever happens to individual objects, that recognition may be the most lasting change of all.

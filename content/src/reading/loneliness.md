[A] Loneliness is usually discussed as an emotional experience, something painful but private. Over the past two decades, however, researchers have built up evidence that persistent loneliness is also associated with physical health problems, including heart disease, weakened immunity and a higher risk of dementia in older age. Some public health bodies now describe it as a risk factor comparable to well-known lifestyle risks.

[B] It is important to distinguish loneliness from being alone. Many people live by themselves and feel perfectly content, while others feel isolated in a busy family or office. Loneliness is the gap between the social connection a person wants and the connection they actually have. That is why it can affect people of any age, including students who have just left home and young parents at home with a baby.

[C] Why should a feeling damage the body? One explanation is that humans evolved in groups, where being cut off from others was genuinely dangerous. Chronic loneliness may keep the body in a state of mild alarm, raising stress hormones and disturbing sleep. Lonely people may also be less likely to exercise, eat well or seek medical help, because no one encourages them to do so.

[D] Responses have taken several forms. In some countries, doctors can now refer patients to community activities, such as walking groups, choirs or volunteering, a practice sometimes called social prescribing. Cities have experimented with benches where strangers are invited to talk, and with housing schemes that mix students and older residents. A few governments have even appointed a minister with responsibility for loneliness.

[E] Technology is an ambiguous part of the picture. Video calls and online communities can keep people in touch across distances, and were a lifeline for many during periods of lockdown. Yet heavy use of social media has been linked with feelings of isolation in some studies, perhaps because comparing ourselves with others' carefully edited lives makes our own seem empty.

[F] There is no single cure, partly because loneliness has so many causes. But recognising it as a health issue, rather than a personal weakness, may make it easier for people to admit that they are struggling, and that may be the first step towards reconnecting.

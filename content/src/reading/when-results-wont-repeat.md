[A] Science depends on a simple promise: if you follow the same method, you should get the same result. A finding that only one laboratory can produce is, at best, a curiosity. Yet over the past two decades, attempts to repeat published studies in fields from psychology to cancer biology have frequently failed. The problem has become known as the replication crisis, or, more cautiously, the reproducibility debate.

[B] In 2016 the journal Nature published the results of an online survey of more than 1,500 researchers. Just over half agreed that there was a significant crisis of reproducibility, and a large majority reported that they had, at some point, failed to reproduce another scientist's experiment; many had even failed to reproduce their own. Yet the same respondents were not pessimistic about science as a whole. Most still trusted much of the published work in their field, and relatively few thought that a failed replication automatically meant the original result was wrong.

[C] This apparent contradiction makes sense once we look at the causes. Some failures to replicate have innocent explanations: an experiment may depend on subtle details of equipment, materials or technique that the original paper did not describe. Others reflect genuine weaknesses in how research is done. Respondents to the survey most often pointed to selective reporting and the pressure to publish. Studies with small samples and low statistical power are also more likely to produce results that look impressive by chance and then disappear when others try to repeat them.

[D] The incentives of academic careers can make these problems worse. Researchers are rewarded for publishing novel, positive findings in prestigious journals. A study that confirms someone else's result, or that finds nothing at all, is harder to publish and rarely advances a career. As a result, the published literature can over-represent striking findings, while the less exciting attempts that would put them in context remain unpublished in "file drawers".

[E] Over the past decade, many reforms have been proposed and tried. In pre-registration, researchers publicly state their hypotheses and analysis plans before collecting data, which makes it harder to change the question after seeing the results. Some journals now accept papers on the basis of their design rather than their findings, so that a well-planned study is published whether or not it produces an exciting result. Sharing data and code allows others to check analyses, and large collaborative projects repeat key experiments across many laboratories at once.

[F] It is worth stressing what the reproducibility debate does not show. It does not mean that science is unreliable in general, or that every surprising result is false. Self-correction is the method working as intended: findings are tested, some do not survive, and confidence is adjusted. The real risk lies elsewhere — in a culture that treats a single published study as settled fact, whether in a laboratory, a newsroom or a government department.

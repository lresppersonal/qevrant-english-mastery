[A] Few psychology experiments are as well known as the "marshmallow test". In studies run at Stanford University from the 1960s, the psychologist Walter Mischel and his colleagues offered young children a choice: they could eat one treat immediately, or wait alone in a room for several minutes and receive two. Some children ate the treat almost at once; others sang, covered their eyes or turned away to resist temptation. Follow-up studies years later reported that children who had waited longer tended to have better outcomes as adolescents, including higher test scores.

[B] The findings became a cultural phenomenon. They were cited in books on success, in parenting advice and in school programmes designed to teach self-control. The appeal was obvious. The idea that a single, simple measure of willpower in early childhood could predict later achievement suggested a clear lesson: teach children to delay gratification, and their futures will improve.

[C] Scientists, however, had long been aware of the limitations of the original follow-ups. The samples were small and drawn largely from the children of staff and students at a prestigious university, a group far from representative of the wider population. In 2018, Tyler Watts, Greg Duncan and Haonan Quan published a conceptual replication in the journal Psychological Science. Using data from a much larger and more diverse group of children, they examined whether the ability to wait at around the age of four predicted achievement at fifteen.

[D] They did find an association, but it was considerably weaker than earlier work had suggested. More importantly, once they took into account factors such as family background, early cognitive ability and the home environment, the link shrank substantially. In other words, a child's ability to wait seemed to reflect, at least in part, the circumstances in which the child was growing up. A child from a household where promises are reliably kept has good reason to trust that a second treat will arrive; a child from a less predictable environment may reasonably take what is on offer now.

[E] It would be a mistake to conclude that self-control is irrelevant. Other research continues to find links between self-regulation and later outcomes, and some replications have reported that the marshmallow test retains a degree of predictive value. The more modest lesson concerns interpretation. A behaviour that looks like a fixed personal trait may partly be a rational response to the environment. Interventions that focus only on training individual willpower may therefore have less effect than those that address the conditions shaping children's choices.

[F] The story of the marshmallow test also illustrates how science is supposed to work. A striking result attracts attention; later researchers test it with better data; the conclusion is refined. The problem lies less with the original scientists than with the way their findings travelled beyond the laboratory, losing their caveats along the way. By the time the result reached popular culture, a cautious correlation had become a confident law of human destiny.

[A] During the Second World War, military planners faced a difficult question. Bombers returning from missions were full of bullet holes, and adding armour could save lives. But armour is heavy, so it could not be added everywhere. The obvious solution was to reinforce the areas where the returning aircraft had been hit most often. A statistician called Abraham Wald, working for a research group in New York, is widely credited with pointing out the flaw in this reasoning.

[B] The planes being examined were only the ones that had survived. Aircraft hit in other places, such as the engines, had not come back at all, so their damage never appeared in the data. The holes on the returning planes showed where a plane could be hit and still fly home. The armour, Wald argued, should go where the survivors had no holes. The story is often simplified in retelling, but the principle it illustrates has a name: survivorship bias, the error of drawing conclusions only from cases that made it through a selection process.

[C] Business advice is particularly vulnerable to this error. Bookshops are full of biographies of successful founders, and online articles analyse the morning routines of billionaires. The message is usually that their habits explain their success: they took bold risks, dropped out of university, or trusted their instincts. What these stories leave out is the much larger group of people who did exactly the same things and failed. Their companies closed quietly, and nobody wrote a book about them.

[D] Once you notice the bias, it appears everywhere. If we study only companies that have lasted a hundred years, we may conclude that their culture made them resilient, without knowing whether many failed firms had the same culture. Investment funds that performed badly are often closed or merged, so the average performance of the funds that remain looks better than the true average. Even the belief that old buildings were "built to last" may partly reflect the fact that badly built ones have already been demolished.

[E] None of this means that successful people have nothing to teach us. The problem is the method, not the source. To learn what causes success, we need to compare winners with losers who started from similar positions. Researchers try to do this by following a whole group over time, including those who drop out, rather than looking backwards from the end. In practical terms, it means asking an awkward question whenever we hear an inspiring story: how many people did the same thing and are not here to tell us about it?

[F] That question is uncomfortable because it removes some of the magic from success. It suggests that luck, timing and starting conditions matter more than many stories admit. But it also offers a kind of protection. A founder who understands survivorship bias is less likely to bet everything on a strategy simply because it worked for someone famous, and more likely to look for evidence that includes the failures.

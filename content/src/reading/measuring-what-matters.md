[A] When politicians boast about a growing economy, they are almost always referring to gross domestic product: the total value of goods and services produced in a country over a period of time. GDP is an extraordinarily useful statistic. It allows comparisons between countries and across decades, and changes in it signal booms and recessions. But it is also routinely treated as something it was never designed to be: a measure of national well-being.

[B] The limitations are well known to economists. GDP counts market activity, so unpaid work such as caring for children or elderly relatives does not appear in it, however valuable it is. It records spending without asking what the spending is for: rebuilding after a flood or treating the victims of pollution adds to GDP just as building a new school does. It says nothing about how income is distributed, so an economy can grow while most households see no improvement. And it treats the depletion of forests, fisheries and fossil fuels as income rather than as the running down of a stock of natural wealth.

[C] None of these criticisms is new. Robert F. Kennedy famously criticised the measure in a 1968 speech, and the debate gained official momentum when the French government set up a commission led by the economists Joseph Stiglitz, Amartya Sen and Jean-Paul Fitoussi. Its 2009 report argued that over-reliance on GDP had given policymakers a distorted picture, and recommended a broader "dashboard" of indicators covering material living standards, health, education, inequality, security, social connections, the environment and people's own evaluations of their lives.

[D] Since then, the "beyond GDP" agenda has moved from academic discussion into official statistics. The OECD launched its Better Life Initiative, and a follow-up report by an expert group linked to the original commission argued that focusing on the wrong indicators had contributed to inadequate policy responses to the 2008 financial crisis. National statistical offices now publish measures of well-being alongside traditional economic data, and the United Nations' Sustainable Development Goals set targets that go well beyond output.

[E] Critics of the dashboard approach raise practical objections. A single number is easy to communicate and hard to manipulate; dozens of indicators can be selectively quoted, and governments may highlight whichever ones look favourable. Combining very different dimensions — life expectancy, air quality, trust in institutions — into a single index requires value judgements about their relative importance, and reasonable people disagree. Subjective measures such as life satisfaction may also be influenced by culture and by how questions are asked.

[F] The defenders of broader measurement rarely argue that GDP should be abolished. Their point is about attention. As Stiglitz has put it, what we measure affects what we do. If a government is judged mainly by growth, it will pursue growth, even when the costs fall on health, nature or future generations. A richer set of indicators cannot settle political arguments about what matters most, but it can ensure that those arguments are conducted with the full picture in view.

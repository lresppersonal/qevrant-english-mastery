[A] For most of the twentieth century, cities treated old industrial buildings as obstacles. Factories, warehouses and power stations that had lost their original purpose were usually demolished, and something new was built in their place. Today the opposite approach, known as adaptive reuse, is becoming mainstream. Instead of knocking a building down, designers keep its structure and give it a new function: a mill becomes flats, a railway shed becomes a market, a church becomes a library.

[B] One of the best-known examples is in London. Bankside Power Station, designed by Sir Giles Gilbert Scott and built in stages after the Second World War, stopped generating electricity in 1981 and stood empty for years, regularly threatened with demolition. In the mid-1990s the Tate gallery chose it as the home for a new museum of modern art, and the Swiss architects Herzog & de Meuron converted it. Tate Modern opened in 2000. The huge turbine hall, once filled with machinery, became a public space for large installations, and the building's industrial character became part of its appeal rather than something to hide.

[C] The environmental argument for reuse is simple but often overlooked. When we think about the carbon footprint of a building, we usually think about the energy needed to heat, cool and light it. However, a large share of a building's lifetime emissions comes from producing materials such as cement and steel and from construction itself. This so-called embodied carbon is released before anyone moves in. Demolishing a sound structure throws that investment away and creates waste, while a new building must "pay back" its own embodied carbon over many years before it becomes more efficient than the one it replaced.

[D] There are social reasons, too. Familiar buildings give neighbourhoods a sense of continuity and identity. Residents who have watched a local landmark decay often feel strongly about what happens to it, and successful projects frequently start with community campaigns. Reused buildings can also offer qualities that are expensive to create from scratch: high ceilings, thick walls, large windows and generous spaces that suit studios, schools and workplaces.

[E] Adaptive reuse is not always straightforward, however. Old buildings may contain hazardous materials, fail modern fire and accessibility standards, or be poorly insulated. Converting them can be technically complex and, in some cases, more expensive than building new. There is also the question of what to keep. Designers must decide which features carry the building's character and which can be changed, and conservation rules may limit their choices. Critics point out that celebrated conversions sometimes attract wealthier newcomers, contributing to rising rents in surrounding areas.

[F] Despite these challenges, many architects now argue that demolition should be the last option rather than the first. Some cities ask developers to justify why an existing building cannot be adapted before permission to demolish it is granted. The question is shifting from "Is this building worth saving?" to "Can we afford not to save it?" For a profession under pressure to cut emissions, the most sustainable material may be the one that is already standing.

[A] Few products seem as ordinary as a plain cotton T-shirt. Yet the one you are wearing may have travelled further than you have this year. Its cotton might have been grown in one country, spun into yarn in a second, knitted and dyed in a third, sewn in a fourth and finally shipped to a warehouse in a fifth before being sold online.

[B] This long supply chain exists because each stage is carried out wherever it is cheapest or most efficient. Cotton grows best in warm climates with plenty of water or irrigation. Spinning and weaving are highly mechanised, so they tend to be located where energy is cheap and factories are large. Sewing, by contrast, still depends heavily on human hands, and is therefore concentrated in countries where wages are low.

[C] Supporters of globalisation argue that this division of labour has made clothing far more affordable and created millions of jobs in developing economies. For many workers, often young women moving from rural areas, a factory job offers a regular income and a degree of independence that farming did not. Several countries have used the garment industry as a first step towards more advanced manufacturing.

[D] The costs, however, are significant. Wages in the sewing stage are frequently barely enough to live on, and working conditions vary widely. Serious factory accidents have drawn international attention to unsafe buildings and weak inspections. The environmental impact is also considerable: growing cotton can require large amounts of water and pesticides, dyeing can pollute rivers, and transporting goods across oceans adds to carbon emissions.

[E] Consumers increasingly ask where their clothes come from, and many brands now publish lists of their suppliers or join certification schemes. Critics warn, though, that transparency at the top of the chain does not always reach the subcontractors further down, where problems are hardest to see. Others argue that the most effective change would come from buying fewer, longer-lasting clothes, an idea that sits uncomfortably with the business model of fast fashion.

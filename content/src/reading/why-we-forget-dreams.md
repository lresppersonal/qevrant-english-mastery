[A] Almost everyone dreams, even people who say they never do. Sleep scientists have found that if you wake people up during certain stages of sleep, most of them can describe a dream. But if the same people wake up naturally a few hours later, they often remember nothing at all. So where do the dreams go?

[B] One simple explanation is timing. Our longest and most story-like dreams usually happen in the second half of the night. If we wake up slowly, or our alarm goes off in a different stage of sleep, the dream may already be over. In that case, there is nothing fresh to remember.

[C] Another explanation is about the brain itself. During sleep, some of the chemicals that help us store new memories are at lower levels than during the day. The brain may be busy with other work, such as sorting information from the previous day. As a result, a dream can feel very real while it is happening but leave almost no trace afterwards.

[D] Attention also matters. When we wake up, our mind quickly turns to the day ahead: the time, the weather, a meeting, a message on our phone. Every new thought pushes the dream further away. Researchers have noticed that people who lie still for a minute and think about their dream before doing anything else remember much more.

[E] Some people keep a dream diary next to their bed and write down a few words as soon as they wake up. After a few weeks, many of them say they remember dreams more often. Nobody is completely sure why this works, but it suggests that remembering dreams is partly a habit that we can learn.

[F] Is it a problem if we forget? Probably not. Scientists still disagree about what dreams are for, and forgetting them seems to be completely normal. But for anyone who is curious about their own mind, a pen and a notebook may be the easiest place to start.

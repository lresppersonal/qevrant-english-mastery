[A] A large study of stories shared on a major social network, published in 2018, reached a striking conclusion: false news tended to spread further, faster and more widely than true news. Crucially, the researchers found that this was driven mainly by people rather than automated accounts. Humans, it seemed, were more willing to pass on falsehoods.

[B] One explanation is novelty. False stories are, almost by definition, more surprising than true ones, because they are not limited by what actually happened. Surprising information attracts attention and gives the person who shares it a kind of social reward: they appear well informed and first with the news. False stories also tend to provoke strong emotions such as fear, anger or disgust, and emotionally charged content is shared more often.

[C] Our own reasoning plays a part too. People are more likely to believe and share claims that fit what they already think, a tendency known as confirmation bias. When a story confirms our views about a group or a politician, we may not stop to check it. Repetition matters as well: hearing a claim several times makes it feel more familiar, and familiar statements are more easily mistaken for true ones.

[D] Platforms have tried a range of responses, including labelling disputed posts, reducing the visibility of content flagged by fact-checkers and removing accounts that spread coordinated campaigns. Each approach has drawbacks. Labels may be ignored, or even increase attention; removal raises concerns about censorship and about who decides what is true.

[E] Some researchers argue that the most promising approach is to change how people share rather than what they see. In experiments, simply asking users to think about whether a headline is accurate before sharing reduced the spread of false content. Others favour "prebunking": teaching people in advance about common manipulation techniques, such as fake experts or emotional language, so that they recognise them later.

[F] None of these solutions is complete. False information has existed as long as communication itself, and technology has mainly changed its speed and scale. The lasting defence may be less a technical fix than a habit of mind: pausing, checking and being willing to admit that a story we like might not be true.

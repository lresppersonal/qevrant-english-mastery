[A] Power, in its most familiar form, is the ability to make others do what they would not otherwise do. States have traditionally exercised it through coercion — the threat or use of force — and through inducement, such as trade deals, aid and sanctions. At the end of the Cold War, the Harvard political scientist Joseph Nye argued that this picture was incomplete. There was a third way to obtain the outcomes one wants: to make others want the same outcomes. He called it soft power.

[B] Soft power, in Nye's account, rests on attraction rather than pressure. Its resources are a country's culture, where others find it appealing; its political values, when it lives up to them at home and abroad; and its foreign policies, when others regard them as legitimate. A state whose universities draw students from around the world, whose films and music are widely enjoyed, and whose institutions are admired may find that other governments and publics are predisposed to cooperate with it, without being bribed or threatened.

[C] The concept proved enormously influential. Governments that had never used the phrase began to talk about "nation branding", cultural diplomacy and global rankings of soft power. Scholarship programmes, international broadcasting, film industries and even national football teams were reframed as strategic assets. In one sense this was a vindication of Nye's argument; in another, it threatened to empty the concept of meaning. If everything that makes a country look good counts as soft power, the term risks becoming a synonym for reputation.

[D] Critics raise deeper objections. First, attraction is difficult to measure and harder still to link to specific outcomes. Admiring a country's music does not necessarily mean supporting its foreign policy; millions of people consume the culture of states whose governments they distrust. Second, much of a country's appeal lies outside the control of its government. Popular culture, independent universities and civil society generate attraction precisely because they are not instruments of the state; once they are openly deployed as tools of policy, they may lose the credibility that made them attractive. Nye himself acknowledged that soft power is harder for governments to wield than hard power, because so many of its resources are beyond their direct command.

[E] A further complication is that soft power can be squandered quickly. Policies perceived as hypocritical — preaching human rights while violating them, or championing free trade while practising protectionism — erode the very legitimacy on which attraction depends. In an age of instant global media, the gap between a country's professed values and its conduct is visible to audiences everywhere, and credibility, as Nye later observed, becomes the scarcest resource.

[F] None of this makes the concept useless. Its enduring value lies in drawing attention to the fact that legitimacy affects the cost of achieving one's goals. A state that is trusted needs fewer threats and fewer payments to gain cooperation. Soft power is best understood not as a separate instrument, to be used instead of military or economic strength, but as a condition that makes all forms of power cheaper — or, when it is lost, considerably more expensive.

[A] In most democracies, voting is a right that citizens are free to use or ignore. In a minority of countries, including Australia, Belgium and several in Latin America, it is also a legal duty. Australia introduced compulsory voting for federal elections in the 1920s, and turnout there regularly exceeds ninety per cent, far higher than in many countries where voting is voluntary.

[B] Supporters argue that compulsory voting produces governments that represent the whole population rather than the most motivated part of it. Where voting is optional, turnout is often lowest among younger, poorer and less educated citizens, so politicians have an incentive to focus on older, wealthier groups who reliably vote. Requiring everyone to participate, the argument goes, forces parties to appeal to the centre and to address the concerns of people they might otherwise ignore.

[C] There are practical benefits too. Parties spend less money persuading their supporters to turn up and can concentrate on persuading undecided voters. Some researchers also suggest that compulsory systems reduce the influence of extreme views, because the electorate is broader than the activists who dominate low-turnout elections.

[D] Opponents see a fundamental contradiction: a democracy that compels people to take part is restricting the very freedom it claims to protect. Choosing not to vote, they argue, can itself be a political statement. Others worry that forcing uninterested citizens to vote adds random or uninformed choices to the result. In practice, most compulsory systems require only that people attend or return a ballot; they may still spoil it or leave it blank.

[E] The penalties involved are usually small fines, and enforcement varies. What seems to matter most is the norm: once voting is expected of everyone, it becomes a habit, and many citizens in such countries say they would continue to vote even if the law changed. Whether that habit could be created elsewhere without compulsion remains an open question.

[A] Philosophers have long used the "trolley problem" to probe moral intuitions. A runaway tram will kill five people unless you divert it onto a track where it will kill one. Should you intervene? For decades the scenario was a classroom puzzle. The prospect of autonomous vehicles has turned it, at least in the eyes of some researchers, into an engineering question: if a crash is unavoidable, whom should a self-driving car be programmed to protect?

[B] To explore public attitudes, a team led by researchers at the MIT Media Lab built the Moral Machine, a multilingual online platform that presented visitors with dilemmas of this kind. Should the car swerve to spare pedestrians at the cost of its passengers? Should it favour the young over the old, the many over the few, people crossing legally over those crossing against the signal? The platform gathered some forty million decisions from millions of participants in more than two hundred countries and territories, and the results were published in Nature in 2018.

[C] Some preferences were remarkably consistent across the world: respondents tended to favour sparing humans over animals, more lives over fewer, and younger people over older ones. Other preferences varied considerably. The researchers identified broad clusters of countries whose answers differed in systematic ways, and found that some of these differences correlated with cultural and institutional characteristics, such as the strength of the rule of law or the degree of economic inequality.

[D] The study's authors presented their findings as a contribution to public debate, not as a set of instructions. Even so, the experiment raises difficult questions about the relationship between what people prefer and what is right. A majority preference is not, in itself, a moral justification; history offers many examples of widely shared views that are now regarded as unjust. Some preferences revealed by such surveys — for instance, valuing certain categories of people over others — would conflict with principles of equal treatment embedded in law in many countries. Germany's ethics commission on automated driving, for example, recommended that systems should not distinguish between people on the basis of personal features such as age.

[E] Critics also question whether the scenarios capture anything real. In practice, a vehicle facing an emergency rarely has certain knowledge of the outcomes of each option; it must act under uncertainty, in fractions of a second, with imperfect sensors. Engineers point out that most safety gains come not from resolving dramatic dilemmas but from avoiding them altogether — through cautious speeds, better braking and more reliable detection. On this view, the trolley problem is a distraction that makes machine ethics seem more exotic, and more tractable, than it is.

[F] Yet the experiment's value may lie precisely in what it exposes. Automated systems will embody choices about risk, whether or not those choices are made explicit. Somebody decides how cautiously a car should drive near a school, how it should balance the safety of its occupants against that of cyclists, and who bears responsibility when it fails. Crowdsourcing cannot settle these questions. What it can do is show how differently people answer them, and remind us that leaving the decisions to engineers alone is itself a moral choice.

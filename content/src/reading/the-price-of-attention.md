[A] In 1971, long before smartphones or social networks, the economist and cognitive scientist Herbert Simon made an observation that now seems prophetic. In an information-rich world, he argued, information consumes something: the attention of its recipients. A wealth of information therefore creates a poverty of attention. The design problem of the future, Simon suggested, would not be how to supply more information, but how to help people decide what to ignore.

[B] Half a century later, the scarcity Simon described has become the basis of an entire economy. Many of the most widely used digital services are free at the point of use. Their revenue comes from advertising, and advertising pays for attention: the more time users spend on a platform, and the more precisely their interests can be predicted, the more valuable that platform becomes. In this sense, as critics often put it, the user is not the customer but the product being sold.

[C] This business model shapes design. Features such as infinite scrolling, autoplay, notifications and personalised recommendation feeds are not neutral conveniences; they are engineered to reduce the friction that might otherwise lead a user to stop. Recommendation systems learn which content holds each person's attention longest. Because emotionally arousing material — outrage, fear, novelty — tends to perform well on these measures, some researchers worry that platforms systematically amplify it, with consequences for public debate as well as for individual well-being.

[D] The evidence on harm is, however, contested. Studies of social media use and mental health have produced mixed results, and many associations are small. It is difficult to separate cause from correlation: anxious people may use their phones more, rather than phones making people anxious. Defenders of the platforms point out that users derive real value from them — information, connection, entertainment — and that "attention" has always been the currency of newspapers, radio and television, which also competed for audiences with dramatic headlines.

[E] What may be new is the scale and precision of the competition. A newspaper cannot rearrange its pages for each reader in real time; a feed can. Individual users face teams of engineers and data scientists whose explicit goal is to increase engagement, and the asymmetry of knowledge between the two sides is considerable. This is why some reformers argue that responsibility cannot rest on personal self-discipline alone, any more than healthy eating can be achieved purely through individual willpower in an environment saturated with cheap, heavily marketed food.

[F] Proposed responses vary widely. Some focus on regulation: limiting data collection, banning certain design features for children, or requiring platforms to offer feeds that are not driven by engagement. Others emphasise design ethics within the industry, or tools that help users set limits. Still others return to Simon's original insight: in an age of abundance, the scarce skill is not finding information but filtering it. Teaching that skill — deciding what deserves our attention — may be one of the central tasks of education in the twenty-first century.

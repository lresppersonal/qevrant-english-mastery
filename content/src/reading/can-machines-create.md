[A] Not long ago, creativity was often described as the one thing machines would never be able to do. Computers could calculate faster than any human and beat champions at chess, but writing a moving story or painting an original picture seemed to require something uniquely human. Today, generative software can produce essays, songs and illustrations on request, and the old certainty has disappeared.

[B] These systems work by analysing enormous quantities of existing text, images or sound and learning the statistical patterns within them. When asked to produce something new, they generate output that follows those patterns. The results can be impressive: fluent, stylish and, at times, genuinely surprising. Yet critics argue that recombining what already exists, however skilfully, is not the same as creating.

[C] Defenders reply that human creativity also builds on what came before. Writers absorb the books they have read; composers borrow forms and harmonies from their predecessors. Very few ideas appear from nowhere. On this view, the difference between human and machine creativity is one of degree rather than kind.

[D] Psychologists often define creativity as producing something that is both novel and valuable. Machines can clearly produce novelty. Value is harder, because it depends on judgement: someone must decide that a piece of work is meaningful, beautiful or useful. At present, that judgement still comes from people, who choose the prompts, select among many outputs and decide what to keep.

[E] The rise of these tools has also raised practical concerns. Artists and authors have asked whether their work was used to train systems without their permission, and courts in several countries are considering how copyright law applies. Teachers worry about students submitting machine-written essays, while some employers see opportunities to cut costs in design and marketing.

[F] Perhaps the most useful question is not whether machines are creative, but how they change human creativity. Like photography in the nineteenth century, which pushed painters away from realism and towards new styles, generative tools may push people towards the kinds of work that depend most on personal experience, intention and taste.

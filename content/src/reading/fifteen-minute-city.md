[A] The concept is easy to state. In a fifteen-minute city, residents can reach most of their everyday needs, including work, schools, shops, healthcare and green space, within a quarter of an hour on foot or by bicycle. The idea, popularised in the late 2010s by the academic Carlos Moreno and adopted by the mayor of Paris, draws on much older traditions of compact, mixed-use neighbourhoods.

[B] For most of the twentieth century, planners in many countries did the opposite. Homes, offices and shopping areas were separated into different zones and connected by roads. This model suited the car, but it also meant long commutes, heavy traffic and town centres that emptied after office hours. Supporters of the fifteen-minute city argue that reversing this separation would cut emissions, improve health and strengthen local communities.

[C] In practice, the changes are often modest. Schoolyards may be opened to the public at weekends, streets near schools closed to cars at certain times, cycle lanes added and planning rules adjusted so that small shops and clinics can open in residential areas. None of this prevents anyone from driving; it aims to make driving less necessary.

[D] Nevertheless, the idea has attracted fierce opposition. Some critics see it as a disguised attempt to restrict personal freedom, especially when it is combined with traffic-control schemes that limit car access to certain streets. Others raise more practical objections: in sprawling suburbs and rural areas, the density needed to support local services simply does not exist, and building it would take decades.

[E] There are also concerns about fairness. If a neighbourhood becomes more pleasant to live in, property prices may rise, pushing out the lower-income residents the plan was supposed to help. Planners therefore increasingly argue that the fifteen-minute city must be accompanied by affordable housing policies.

[F] Much of the controversy may come from the name, which sounds like a rule rather than an aspiration. Few supporters claim that every city can be redesigned within fifteen-minute boundaries. The underlying question is more basic: should cities be planned primarily around cars, or around the people who live in them?

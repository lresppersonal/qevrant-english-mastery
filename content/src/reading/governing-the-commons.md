[A] Imagine a pasture open to every herder in a village. Each herder gains the full benefit of adding one more animal, while the cost of overgrazing is shared among everyone. The individually rational choice, repeated by each herder, leads to a collectively disastrous result: the grass is destroyed and all are ruined. This parable, popularised in a 1968 essay as "the tragedy of the commons", became one of the most influential ideas in environmental thinking. It seemed to explain overfishing, deforestation and the depletion of groundwater with unsettling clarity.

[B] The policy conclusion appeared equally clear. If individuals cannot be trusted to restrain themselves, shared resources must either be placed under the control of the state, which can impose limits, or divided into private property, so that each owner bears the cost of his or her own overuse. For much of the twentieth century these two options — government regulation or privatisation — framed almost every debate about natural resources.

[C] The political scientist Elinor Ostrom challenged this dichotomy not with a rival theory but with evidence. Studying fisheries, forests, irrigation systems and mountain pastures in many parts of the world, she and her colleagues documented communities that had managed shared resources sustainably, in some cases for centuries, without relying on either a central authority or private ownership. In 2009 she became the first woman to receive the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences, awarded for her analysis of economic governance, especially the commons.

[D] What distinguished the successful cases was not altruism but institutions: rules, often unwritten, that the users themselves had devised and enforced. Ostrom identified recurring features. The boundaries of the resource and the group entitled to use it were clearly defined. Rules were adapted to local conditions and could be modified by the people affected. Users monitored one another, and sanctions for breaking the rules were graduated, starting mildly and escalating for repeat offenders. Cheap and accessible mechanisms existed for resolving disputes, and external authorities recognised the community's right to organise itself.

[E] These findings did not overturn the tragedy of the commons so much as qualify it. The original parable assumes that herders cannot communicate or make binding agreements. Where those conditions hold — among strangers, for instance, or when a resource is too vast for anyone to monitor — overuse remains a real danger. Ostrom's point was that such conditions are an empirical question rather than a law of nature, and that well-meaning interventions which replace functioning local arrangements with distant regulation or hasty privatisation can destroy the very trust on which cooperation depends.

[F] The relevance of her work has, if anything, grown. Many of the most pressing problems of the present — climate change, the oceans, even shared digital infrastructure — are commons on a global scale, where the features that made small communities successful are hardest to reproduce. Ostrom herself warned against "panaceas", single solutions applied regardless of context. Her legacy is less a formula than a disposition: a willingness to look at how people actually cooperate before deciding that they cannot.

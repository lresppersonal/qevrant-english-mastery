[A] For most of the twentieth century, conservation meant management. Reserves were fenced, grass was cut, invasive plants were removed and particular species were protected, often at considerable expense. Rewilding takes a strikingly different approach. Its central idea is that, given enough space and a few key ingredients, natural processes can repair ecosystems more effectively than human planning can.

[B] The most famous example is the return of grey wolves to Yellowstone National Park in the United States in the mid-1990s, after an absence of about seventy years. Supporters argue that the wolves did far more than reduce the number of elk. Because the elk began to avoid open valleys where they were vulnerable, young trees along riverbanks had a chance to grow. Their roots stabilised the banks, and new habitats appeared for birds and beavers. Scientists call such a chain of effects a trophic cascade, although some researchers now believe the Yellowstone story has been simplified, and that drought, hunting and other factors also played a part.

[C] In Europe, rewilding has taken a different form, partly because large predators and dense human populations are an uneasy combination. Projects have reintroduced beavers, whose dams slow down rivers and reduce flooding downstream, and grazing animals such as wild cattle and horses, which keep landscapes open in a more varied way than mowing. In some regions, farmland abandoned as rural populations decline has simply been left to recover on its own.

[D] Critics raise several objections. Farmers worry about livestock losses when predators return, and compensation schemes are not always generous or quick. Others question whether there is any meaningful "natural" state to return to, since landscapes have been shaped by people for thousands of years. Choosing a baseline, whether before industrialisation, before farming or before humans arrived, is ultimately a value judgement rather than a scientific fact.

[E] There are economic arguments on both sides as well. Rewilded areas can attract tourists and create jobs in guiding and hospitality, sometimes in regions where agriculture is no longer profitable. Yet these benefits rarely reach everyone equally, and residents who depended on traditional land use may feel that decisions are being made by distant organisations with little understanding of local life.

[F] Perhaps the most persuasive case for rewilding is a modest one. It does not claim that nature can solve every environmental problem, nor that human management is always harmful. Instead, it suggests that, in the right places, stepping back may achieve more than intervening. The challenge is to decide, together with the people who live there, where those places are.
